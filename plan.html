Spatial Memory · Plan

Why we run two studies, what happens in each, and what we measure. Task details may still move; the logic is locked. Academic spec in the Full Doc §6.

The whole plan in one breath: the paper claims grounding can move into a shared room across time. Study 1 shows people will actually put knowledge into the room when saying it costs one breath. Study 2 shows a later stranger can take that knowledge out again, and that the credit belongs to the structure, not the pinning. A technical check in between shows the pipeline connecting the two is real.

1The goal, and why it takes two studies

The claim has two sides that can never be in the room at the same time, because the medium is about different times. So the test is cut in two. The speaking side: when leaving word costs one breath instead of a composed message, the small situated knowledge that today goes unsaid gets said. The reading side: a newcomer who was never addressed finds that knowledge where and when it matters, and reads it right, and the thing doing that work is the Memory Graph, not the mere pinning. And underneath both, the honest question: did understanding actually happen, and where exactly does it stop happening. The boundary is reported, not hidden.

One discipline governs everything below: the studies are the storyboard week with the surfaces swapped. Same structures, different props, so the scenario, the studies, and the demo are one paradigm.

2Setup, shared by both studies

The room is our own lab dressed as the scenario's maker lab: the Bambu P2S, the four-camera capture frame over its bench, the shared device shelf, a soldering station, workbenches. Everyone wears a Quest 3 in passthrough. Participants are makerspace-experienced people from outside the lab; labmates only pilot. In Study 1 the capture is wizarded: speaking genuinely records audio and location, but no live pipeline runs behind it. Every session is passthrough-recorded for behavior coding.

3Study 1 · will people say it? (8–10 people, each comes twice)

Goal: prove the speaking side. When a note costs one breath, encounter-class knowledge gets externalized; when it costs pulling out a phone and composing a message, most of it dies.

What happens: each person comes twice, at least a day apart. On one visit they can speak in place; on the other they only have their phone and the lab Slack. Half start with each. Both visits wear the headset, so the only difference is the channel. Each visit is about 40 minutes of real lab work under mild time pressure (collect a print, assemble a bracket, wire a connector, stage a logging phone, clear and calibrate for the next job), and the two visits use different but structurally identical task sets, so nobody meets the same situation twice. The cover story: you are the morning shift, someone you do not know takes over this afternoon; handle anything you notice however you normally would. We never mention recording; saying something stays optional and slightly costly, like life.

What we measure: for each of the nine, did they say it: fully, partly, or not at all. An exit walkthrough ("show me what you noticed") separates did not notice from noticed and stayed silent; only the second counts. Plus the time each record took away from the work, word counts, and workload (NASA-TLX). Two bonus readings: E8 quietly measures whether people give a bump report to the place or to the person (the scenario's Dae-ho moment, measured in the wild), and E9 checks that messages with a known recipient still go to Slack in both visits, so the system is not stealing the channel's job.

What it hands over: everything they said, with location and timestamp, becomes the test corpus for the pipeline check and the raw material of Study 2's room. The "Feeds Study 2" column above is that handoff, planned in advance.

4The pipeline check (no participants)

The question (RQ3): can messy in-flow speech be turned into the typed, placed, linked record the concept assumes, at a correction cost small enough to preserve one-breath capture? Both studies deliberately bypass the live pipeline (Study 1 is wizarded, Study 2 is frozen), so this is the one place the real system is tested. All Study 1 recordings are run through the real pipeline offline: does it split a multi-topic breath into the right cards, put each card in the right place, propose the right audience and urgency, and spot the links (this replaces that, read this first, this answers that)? Scored against two human coders' gold labels, and reported with one honest number: how many fixes per card the author would have had to make at the confirmation step. This is the technical evaluation of the Memory Graph's writer. Anchoring reliability on Quest 3 (does a card resurface in the right spot across sessions) is measured alongside.

5Study 2 · can a stranger pick it up? (16 people, each comes once, two groups of 8)

Goal: Study 1 shows people will put knowledge into the room. Study 2 asks whether a newcomer gets a correct picture back out, and who deserves the credit. Pinning notes where they were spoken already does real work: standing at the thing, a pinned note reads fine. What is on trial is everything beyond pinning: collapsing old versions into the current one, carrying word to the bench where it is needed, filtering by who it is for, putting warnings first. The registered prediction: wherever the task is "stand at the thing and read it," the two versions tie; wherever the room is realistically messy (records piled on one spot, things changed after writing, word needed at another bench, a warning buried in chatter), the full version keeps the newcomer right and the stripped one does not. The tie is registered in advance on purpose: it is what proves the differences come from the graph rather than from pinning.

Design: 16 fresh people, one visit each, cast as a newcomer taking over a project mid-week. They split into two groups of eight, between subjects: nobody sees both versions, because a planted situation teaches its lesson the first time you meet it, and a second room cannot un-teach it. One group uses the full version, the Memory Graph running (old versions collapse, notes travel with projects, each person hears what is for them, warnings order first, "who did this" is answerable). The other group uses the stripped version: the very same notes as dumb pins at the places they were spoken, old versions hanging beside new ones, everything visible to everyone, nothing routed, no ordering, no "anything for me?". Same room, same notes, same paper work order; the software is the only difference.

What makes the room hard: five countable dials. Everything that makes a newcomer's first hour difficult can be counted: how many records have piled up on one spot; how many things changed after the last record was made; how many benches apart the word and the work are; how many projects' notes share a zone; how many people's voices interleave. We set difficulty with these five numbers and nothing else, so every hard moment in the study can be pointed at and named. Time splits into two dials on purpose, because "many records" and "the world moved on" are different problems: a tall pile is hard to integrate even when it is all true; a drifted record is wrong even when it is the only one. Raw note count is not a dial of its own; it is the product of dials 1, 4, and 5, controlled and reported.

How the dials become the task list. Each mechanism of the graph is strained by some dials and untouched by others: turning up crowding cannot make a pile-up harder to read, and no amount of distance strains supersession. Crossing the six mechanisms with the five dials therefore lays out every way this study could be made hard; that grid is the combinatorics made explicit. Seven crossings genuinely strain something, and those seven, numbered 1 to 7 below, are exactly what the study runs, each at one realistic difficulty setting (a busy spot in our lab accumulates 3–5 records a week; a shared bench carries 3–4 projects). ✓ crossings are meaningful but left for later work; — crossings do not strain. We deliberately do not walk any dial up a ladder: the claim is a pattern of where the stripped version breaks, not a threshold, and GKF likewise ran their main experiments at a single point well above threshold.

The note schema. Every note in the room is built to one schema, and the build sheet (prep item 2) fills it in for all ~34: content (the words) · anchor (the exact thing or spot it is pinned to) · kind (an object note stays at its anchor; a project note travels with its project) · audience (broadcast / a project / a named person) · urgency (info / warning) · author (one of five voices) · day (when in the staged week it was spoken) · status (current / superseded / drifted, meaning the world changed after it) · links (supersedes an earlier note; must-read-before an action) · source (Study 1 real / built). The full version renders all of this; the stripped version flattens every property except content and anchor, which is the ablation stated note by note. Below, each note is written as "content" (audience · kind · status), with author and day where they matter. The newcomer is cast onto the Rig project (the capture-rig build); the other projects sharing the room are Drone and Kiosk.

3 The world-moved-on pair · DRIFT-1, DRIFT-2. The note was true when spoken; after the freeze we changed the world, so its status is drifted, and no version can make the newcomer automatically right. The question is whether a visible timestamp and the trail of later activity turn blind trust into a quick check. The registered side-prediction: the stripped group errs confidently, because nothing on a pinned note says it aged.
DRIFT-1, on the scale: "re-zeroed the scale for the resin batch, good to go" (broadcast · object · drifted; Study 1, day 1). After the freeze we offset the tare; a reference weight sits beside the scale. Task: "weigh 20 g of pigment and write the number down." Scored: checks the zero or the reference before trusting; blind acceptance is the error.
DRIFT-2, on the light stand: "the stand is aligned to the tape marks for the A-captures" (Rig · object · drifted; Study 1, day 1). After the freeze we nudge it off the marks. Task: "run the quick capture test." Scored: glances at the marks first.

In the full version each reaches the Rig newcomer at the destination and speaks only to Rig; in the stripped version it hangs where it was said, and the path recordings will show the stripped group simply never met it.

6 The buried warning · WARN-1, at the soldering bench. The scored note: "the USB-C cable at this bench is fraying near the plug, don't yank it" (broadcast · object · warning · current; Study 1). Four info notes hang at the same bench: "the flux pen is nearly out, new ones in the drawer" (broadcast · object · current), "the chisel tip is in the iron" (broadcast · object · current), "kiosk LED strips: solder at 300 °C max" (Kiosk · object · current), "leave the helping hands clamped, they're set for the drone boards" (Drone · object · current). The arrival scene (56 at room scale). The first five minutes: "take a few minutes to find out what you need to know today." Three notes are genuinely for the newcomer: the two Rig project notes above, and one directed handover, "for whoever picks up the rig project: the parts box is under bench 3" (Rig · project · current; built). One hazard exists, the warning at 6. Scored: how many of the three they find, how much irrelevant they open on the way, and time-to-hazard.

Session script: (1) consent, headset familiarization in a neutral zone, 5 min; (2) cover story and the paper work order, ten tasks in a natural route; (3) ARRIVAL, clock running, 5 min; (4) work the order, ~35 min, the wizard logs each behavioral exit live against the action script; (5) late probe kept from the earlier plan: "anything for me?" returns empty; do they trust the nothing or go hunting; (6) retell each scored note in your own words plus confidence, NASA-TLX, exit interview, 10 min. About 55 minutes per person.

Dosage: about 34 notes, a week's worth: roughly 20 task-relevant (the items above, including chain links and the five-author interleave) and 14 realistic noise, other projects' chatter, notes for other people, superseded old versions. The noise is on purpose: filtering can only prove itself against a pile.

What we measure, three layers. Outcomes: for each item, did the action land on the true side, coded on the four-step scale (skipped it · never found what it referred to · found it but read it wrong · got it) by two blind coders against action scripts written before piloting; families pooled, no single-cell significance chasing. Error types are kept (which wrong version they believed, what they missed): they are the raw material for the where-does-the-picture-fail question. Process: consulted-before-acting (the pooling measure, unchanged), navigation path (served by triage · walked past it · dug for it), wasted work (re-checking settled things, extra walking, questions to the experimenter), arrival coverage and precision, time-to-warning. Subjective: retell + confidence per note, never "did you understand"; workload. One registered calibration read: confidence × correctness, with the prediction that the stripped group errs confidently on the DRIFT items, because nothing in a pinned note says it aged.

6The real-teams leg (with YY)

Two or three real pairs use the system in their own work for a few days. No planted tasks, no scripts; we watch where communication breaks and how it gets repaired, coded as breakdown-and-repair episodes plus thematic analysis. The lab studies prove the mechanism; this leg shows it happens outside a script. Scheduled after the demo scenes prove the system runs live.

7Still open

Compensation and recruitment channel; an IRB check on whether the submitted protocol (STU00226786) covers the planted-situation cover stories or needs an amendment; the size of the real-teams leg; the grow-to-12 rule for Study 2; and one design decision to settle before piloting: on the DRIFT items, which mechanism the full system is claimed to help with (timestamp salience vs. visible later activity near the anchor), since the fairness of identical walls in both arms depends on naming it in advance.
