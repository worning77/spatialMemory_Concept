Grounding Theory

The theoretical frame: grounding relocated into space.

People who share physical workspaces at different times of the day — fabrication labs, design studios, research labs — rely on those spaces as rich, structured environments for making and thinking. Yet their communication with one another happens through placeless tools: chat channels, shared docs, message threads. This disconnect means that the spatial context which organizes the process of thinking and making is lost.

Drawing on the theory of Grounding (Clark & Brennan, 1991), collaboration depends on common ground, the shared knowledge, beliefs, and assumptions that collaborators mutually hold and continually update as they work. In a shared physical space, an important part of grounding is communicating who needs to know what, where, and when — matching a piece of situated knowledge to the person who needs it, the place it belongs, and the moment it becomes relevant. Much of what supports this is anchored in the environment itself: where things are, what state they are in, and what was done to them. When collaborators are separated in time and communicate through placeless tools, the who, what, where, and when come unbound from one another. A placeless message then has to carry the completeness of all the parts in its language. If any piece of context is left out, the common ground breaks down, introducing uncertainty and inviting misunderstanding.

Existing approaches to bridging this gap, from AR annotation systems to media spaces, either demand heavy instrumentation or treat space as a simple pin-board for isolated comments, offering anchoring without structured context and process. The core design challenge is to support the construction of common ground across people who work at different times, in different sequences, and through different processes.

We introduce Spatial Memory, an interaction paradigm in which physical space becomes the medium for the collaborative construction of common ground: voice notes left by multiple collaborators over time are anchored to the locations and objects they concern, structured according to project-dependent context and causal relationships between tasks and decisions, and delivered to each collaborator where and when they need them. The space becomes a layered grounding record that can be reviewed, revised, and navigated by place, by person, or by project.
