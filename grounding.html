Project Framing w/ Theory of Grounding

The theoretical frame: grounding relocated into space.

People who share physical workspaces at different times of the day — fabrication labs, design studios, research labs — rely on those spaces as rich, structured environments for making and thinking. Yet their communication with one another happens through placeless tools: chat channels, shared docs, message threads. This disconnect means that the spatial context which organizes the process of thinking and making is lost.

Drawing on the theory of Grounding (Clark & Brennan, 1991), collaboration depends on common ground, the shared knowledge, beliefs, and assumptions that collaborators mutually hold and continually update as they work. In a shared physical space, an important part of grounding is communicating who needs to know what, where, and when — matching a piece of situated knowledge to the person who needs it, the place it belongs, and the moment it becomes relevant. Much of what supports this is anchored in the environment itself: where things are, what state they are in, and what was done to them. When collaborators are separated in time and communicate through placeless tools, the who, what, where, and when come unbound from one another. A placeless message then has to carry the completeness of all the parts in its language. If any piece of context is left out, the common ground breaks down, introducing uncertainty and inviting misunderstanding.

Existing approaches to bridging this gap, from AR annotation systems to media spaces, either demand heavy instrumentation or treat space as a simple pin-board for isolated comments, offering anchoring without structured context and process. The core design challenge is to support the construction of common ground across people who work at different times, in different sequences, and through different processes.

We introduce Spatial Memory, an interaction paradigm in which physical space becomes the medium for the collaborative construction of common ground: voice notes left by multiple collaborators over time are anchored to the locations and objects they concern, structured by place, project, and the relationships the Memory Graph maintains (supersession, audience, and routing), and delivered to each collaborator where and when they need them. The space becomes a layered grounding record that can be reviewed, revised, and navigated by place, by person, or by project.

The Empty Cell: Sequential Copresence

Clark and Brennan characterize a medium by eight constraints it may hold for its participants: copresence, visibility, audibility, cotemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, reviewability, and revisability (p. 141). Their Table 1 (p. 142) tabulates seven media against these constraints, and the table has a hole. Copresence appears exactly once, for face-to-face, where it is bundled with cotemporality; every asynchronous medium they analyze (letters, answering machines, email) is placeless. Same place, different time is a profile the theory can express but never instantiates.

Spatial Memory is that missing row. We call the profile sequential copresence: collaborators share the same physical environment, which is exactly how Clark and Brennan define copresence ("A and B share the same physical environment," p. 141), but they occupy it in sequence rather than at once. The extension is honest about what transfers and what does not. What transfers is copresence's referential payoff: the later reader stands where the note was spoken, so an indicative "this one" keeps its referent (display costs, p. 145) and the context does not have to be imagined from words, which is the burden Clark and Brennan assign to email readers (understanding costs, p. 143). What does not transfer is every affordance that presupposes a simultaneous partner: precise timing, live gesture, monitoring, immediate repair (p. 144). The three substitutions below are this asymmetry turned into design: the first two exploit what transfers, and the third stands in for what does not. Clark and Brennan name the residue precisely: "In a medium such as email, B's lack of response can be highly ambiguous. Did she not get the message, did she get it and not read it, did she read it and choose not to respond, did she not understand it, or what?" (p. 146). That state ambiguity is the boundary the scenario dramatizes and the evaluation tests.

The cell is empty in the theory, not empty of occupants. Replay systems stand in this quadrant: Asynchronous Reality captures physical events and replays them to a later user, and a long line of AR annotation systems pins notes to places. So does the oldest medium of all: the sticky note on the broken machine is this quadrant's folk medium, and it has worked for decades. But all of these carry delivery, not grounding. A replay delivers events with no acceptance phase; a pinned note delivers words with no evidence of understanding, no supersession when it goes stale, no audience, and no repair. In grounding terms, they occupy the place and leave the machinery empty. Spatial Memory is the sticky note given the grounding machinery it never had, and delivery without grounding is precisely the failure the scenario stages.

Relocating grounding: three substitutions

Grounding, in Clark and Brennan a synchronous, two-party achievement, is relocated into a space-anchored, accumulated structure through three substitutions. Each converts a move a live partner would otherwise carry into a property of the record.

Grounding moveSubstitution
choose a recipientanchor to a place: the note attaches to the place, not a named addressee
describe the referentspatial co-location: the reader later stands where it was said, so deixis is restored across time
accept with a live replydeferred acceptance: a later person's action on the world stands in for the reply

The third is load-bearing and fragile: asynchrony removes repair and cannot complete the mutual belief a live reply secures, so we report where grounding lands only one-way. The synchronous version of this relocation is From Speech-to-Spatial (Kim et al., ISMAR 2026), which grounds spoken referents on a live shared view; ours is the asynchronous case. In one line, they are a spatial Zoom and we are a spatial Slack.

Grounding Cost and the Design of Spatial Memory

In the principle of least collaborative effort, Clark and Brennan describe the cost of grounding as the combined effort of the participants, who ground with as little effort as possible for the sake of efficiency. What any grounding technique costs depends on the medium. This variation in grounding cost across media is the mechanism that explains why sharing situated knowledge can be costly in a shared workspace — and why it often goes unsaid — and it specifies what a Spatial Memory system would have to absorb to change that.

In a co-present, real-time exchange, the effort of grounding is shared between two parties and paid as the conversation unfolds. In a space that is shared but asynchronous, there is no live partner to share the effort and no exchange to spread it across: the whole cost falls on the author of the message, for a payoff that goes to someone else, later. When the cost of contributing a small observation exceeds what it appears to be worth to the person paying it, silence is the rational choice. The knowledge is not lost to forgetting or indifference; it is lost to a cost structure that makes communicating it locally irrational.

Which costs apply depends on the medium's profile. Under sequential copresence the whole cost of a contribution falls on the author at capture time, so the design has to drive the author-side costs (formulation, production, start-up) toward zero. Clark and Brennan supply the mechanism for why channels fail here: in media where changing speaker is "a little like starting up a communication from scratch," people "try to do more within a turn" (pp. 144–145). That composed, effortful channel message is exactly what prices encounter-class information out; a one-breath note spoken in place is the counter-design.

Under sequential copresence the eleven costs sort into a division of labor: some are the design brief (drive them toward zero or the note is never spoken), some are erased or destroyed by the profile itself (no design needed, or no design possible), and some become the evaluation's instrument (the substitutions claim to hold them down; the studies measure whether they do):

Read as a ledger: the speaker-side and start-up rows are the design brief, and whether they dropped is what Study 1's externalization rate measures against a channel baseline. The addressee-side rows, understanding, fault, and repair, are the instrument of Study 2: the substitutions claim to hold them down without a live partner, and the seeded traps measure where that claim holds and where the missing repair turn lets a misreading through. Delay and asynchrony belong to neither list, since the profile erases the first and destroys the second; the destroyed one is the boundary the whole paper is honest about.
