Project Framing w/ Workspace Awareness

The theoretical frame: workspace awareness carried across the temporal gap. (Supersedes the grounding spine; see Archive for the previous version.)

The framing in one sentence: a shift-shared workspace poses an awareness problem, not a conversation problem: the arriver needs a correct, current picture of what absent others did (situation awareness, Endsley; workspace awareness, Gutwin & Greenberg); the awareness literature built its techniques for real-time distributed groupware and never operationalized its own past-tense elements (G&G Table II, exactly the questions an arriver asks), and in a sequentially occupied space only feedthrough survives the actor's absence, showing the present without the past; Clark and Brennan enter only as the cost ledger that explains why this awareness goes unsaid today (the whole cost falls on the author, the payoff goes to someone else, so silence is rational); Spatial Memory answers with authored feedthrough and maintained state (one-breath notes carrying history, authorship, and timing; supersession as mechanical awareness maintenance); and the two studies test the two halves (Study 1: does the record get made when the cost drops; Study 2: does structure keep the arriver's picture correct, a GKF-style ablation).

People who share physical workspaces in shifts, in fabrication labs, design studios, and research labs, rely on those spaces as rich, structured environments for making and thinking. The workspace is shared and the equipment is shared, but the people are rarely there at the same time. Every person who walks in faces state changes and dependencies created by people they cannot see: a swapped nozzle, a re-zeroed scale, a rig that must not be touched until tomorrow. Nothing about the current appearance of the room announces any of this.

This is not a conversation problem. There are no long exchanges to support, no misunderstanding to negotiate. It is what the awareness literature calls situation awareness (Endsley, 1995), in its workspace-specific form, workspace awareness (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002): knowing what has happened in the space, who did it, when, and what state things are in now. The arriver's problem is that this knowledge exists, in the head of the person who just left, and the room does not carry it.

Today the carrying is done by placeless channels: chat threads, shared docs, whiteboards by the door. These strip the knowledge from the place and moment it belongs to, and, as the cost ledger below shows, they price small observations out entirely, so most of this awareness is never externalized at all. AR annotation systems anchor content to place but treat space as a pin-board for isolated comments: they deliver words, but keep no state.

We introduce Spatial Memory, an interaction paradigm in which the workspace itself carries its past: voice notes spoken in one breath at the thing they concern, anchored where they were said, structured by the Memory Graph (supersession, audience, routing), and surfaced to each later arriver where and when they become relevant. The space becomes a maintained record of its own history that can be navigated by place, by person, or by project.

Why It Goes Unsaid: The Cost Structure

Clark and Brennan (1991) supply the motivation engine: communication effort is paid against a medium-specific cost structure, and people, following the principle of least collaborative effort, communicate only what the costs make rational. We borrow their cost ledger, not their grounding machinery; the ledger explains why encounter-class awareness never gets externalized today, and it specifies what a Spatial Memory system has to absorb to change that.

In a co-present, real-time exchange, the effort is shared between two parties and paid as the conversation unfolds. In a space that is shared but occupied in sequence, there is no live partner to share the effort: the whole cost falls on the author, at capture time, for a payoff that goes to someone else, later. When the cost of contributing a small observation exceeds what it appears to be worth to the person paying it, silence is the rational choice. The knowledge is not lost to forgetting or indifference; it is lost to a cost structure that makes communicating it locally irrational.

Channels make this worse in a specific, cited way: in media where changing speaker is "a little like starting up a communication from scratch," people "try to do more within a turn" (pp. 144–145). That composed, effortful channel message is exactly what prices encounter-class information out; a one-breath note spoken in place is the counter-design. Under sequential copresence the eleven costs sort into a division of labor: some are the design brief (drive them toward zero or the note is never spoken), some are erased or destroyed by the profile itself, and some become the evaluation's instrument:

The addressee-side rows, understanding, fault, and repair, are the instrument of Study 2: structure claims to keep the arriver's picture correct without a live partner, and the seeded traps measure where that claim holds. Delay and asynchrony belong to neither list: the profile erases the first and destroys the second, and the destroyed one is precisely why the record itself, not any exchange, has to carry the state of the room.

The Phenomenon: Awareness, Not Grounding

Gergle, Kraut and Fussell (2013) showed that shared visual information serves two separable coordination mechanisms, and that most systems and most prior studies confound them. Situation awareness is knowledge of task state: has the piece been placed, has an error occurred, is the partner ready. Conversational grounding is evidence of mutual understanding: seeing the partner move the right piece is a nonverbal acknowledgment. Their experiments dissociate the two by ablating one feature of the medium at a time and reading, from where compensation appears in the process measures, which mechanism that feature was serving.

Spatial Memory sits on the awareness side: an arriver needs a correct picture of the workspace, not a conversation. Nothing in the phenomenon requires acceptance, acknowledgment, or mutual belief; the leaver is gone, and what they left behind either keeps the arriver's picture current and correct or it does not. Gutwin and Greenberg (2002) give this picture its vocabulary: workspace awareness, "the up-to-the-moment understanding of another person's interaction with the shared workspace" (p. 417), a specialization of situation awareness tied to the setting we care about.

The Gap: Awareness Frameworks Assume Real Time

Gutwin and Greenberg's framework is the reference account of workspace awareness, and it is scoped, explicitly and by definition, to people working "at the same time, but from different places" (p. 413): real-time distributed groupware. The temporal bound is inside the definition itself ("up-to-the-moment"). But the framework's own element tables reach further than its scope. Alongside the present-tense elements (who is here, what are they doing, where), their Table II enumerates the elements of workspace awareness relating to the past:

CategoryElementQuestion it answers (verbatim, Table II, p. 422)

Who (past)Presence historyWho was here, and when?
Where (past)Location historyWhere has a person been?
What (past)Action historyWhat has a person been doing?

These are exactly the questions an arriver in a shift-shared lab is asking. And they are the half of the framework that was never built: when the paper turns to interface techniques, it states "We concentrate here on real-time aspects of workspace awareness – elements that answer the who, what, and where questions" (p. 434), and its technique catalogue covers only the present-tense elements. The past half of their own framework is named but never operationalized; that unserved half is the slot Spatial Memory fills.

The framework also predicts why the sequential case is hard. G&G identify three channels through which awareness information is gathered: watching people work (consequential communication), artifacts giving off state (feedthrough), and talk and gesture (intentional communication). Under "a spatial or temporal separation between the artifact and the actor," they note, feedthrough is left as "the only vehicle for information" (pp. 423–424). Sequential copresence is the extreme of that observation: the actor is always absent, so of the three channels only feedthrough survives, and raw physical feedthrough shows the present without the past. An artifact's appearance carries no history, no authorship, and no currency: a nudged camera rig looks exactly like a calibrated one, a dryer mid-run looks idle, a re-zeroed scale looks normal.

The cell is unserved in the literature, not empty of occupants. Replay systems stand in it: Asynchronous Reality captures physical events and replays them to a later user, and a long line of AR annotation systems pins notes to places. So does the oldest medium of all: the sticky note on the broken machine is this quadrant's folk medium, and it has worked for decades. But all of these deliver content without maintaining a picture. A replayed event stream answers "what happened" only by making the arriver watch all of it; a pinned note has no currency, so E7's "out of PLA" sign outlives its own truth; none of them knows who a note is for, whether it has been superseded, or what state the artifact is in now. Spatial Memory is the sticky note given a memory: state, not log.

The Move: Authored Feedthrough, Maintained State

The design follows from the mechanism argument. If raw feedthrough is the only channel that survives the temporal gap, then the system's job is to author into the artifact the information its appearance does not give off. A note spoken in one breath at the thing becomes exactly the Table II elements the appearance lacks: artifact history ("swapped the 0.6 nozzle in for the draft job"), event timing ("started the logging run at 4pm"), authorship, and, through the Memory Graph, currency. What transfers from copresence is the referential payoff: the later reader stands where the note was spoken, so an indicative "this one" keeps its referent and the context does not have to be rebuilt from language, the burden Clark and Brennan assign to email readers (p. 143).

Maintenance is the second half of the move. Gutwin and Greenberg's own characterization of awareness (p. 416) is that "environments change over time, so awareness is knowledge that must be maintained and kept up to date." A log cannot do that; a pinned note cannot do that. The Memory Graph's supersession is awareness maintenance made mechanical: when a later note contradicts an earlier one, the record revises itself, and the arriver's picture tracks the room rather than the room's history of announcements. This is the state-not-log principle on the Concept tab, now with its theoretical job description.

One further element goes beyond what any feedthrough could carry. G&G exclude future-directed elements from their framework because, unlike past and present information, belief about the future "involves inference, extrapolation, and prediction" and cannot be determined from raw perception (p. 421). An authored note is not bound by perception: "epoxy cures 24 hours, do not sand before Thursday morning" hands the arriver a dependency that no amount of looking at the part reveals. Directed and routed notes are the system reaching past the perceptual bound that raw artifacts cannot cross.

What the Studies Test

The two studies keep their architecture (leaver → frozen seeds → arriver) and gain a cleaner reading. Study 1 tests the cost half: when externalizing costs one breath at the thing, do the awareness records that today go unsaid actually get made? The externalization rate against a channel baseline, per encounter type, is the measure. Study 2 tests the structure half, and it now sits squarely in GKF's constraint-ablation tradition: remove one capability (SM-full vs anchored-only, which strips supersession, routing, and triage but keeps anchoring), predict which failures appear, and read the mechanism from where the arriver's picture breaks. The seeded traps are those predicted failures by name: a stale note (the world changed after writing), a superseded pair, a routed dependency, a warning that must surface first. Anchoring alone should preserve reference and still fail on currency; that dissociation is the study's argument.

The encounter pool and traps are being re-derived as cells of a dimension table that crosses G&G's awareness elements with readers, spatial scope, currency, coupling, and urgency; the table and the task derivation live on the Plan tab.
