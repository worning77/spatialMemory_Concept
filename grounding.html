Project Framing w/ Workspace Awareness

The theoretical frame: workspace awareness carried across the temporal gap. (Supersedes the grounding spine; see Archive for the previous version.)

The framing in one sentence: Spatial Memory serves workspaces occupied in sequence (sequential copresence: same place, different time), where every arriver needs a current, correct picture of what happened while they were away (the arriver's questions: how did this come to be in this state, when, who; the past elements of workspace awareness, G&G 2002); with the actor gone only the artifact still speaks, and raw feedthrough shows the present without the past, while the one person who could fill the gap stays silent because externalizing costs them everything and pays them nothing (Clark & Brennan's cost ledger, the design brief); so Spatial Memory authors the missing past into the place (one-breath notes as authored feedthrough) and keeps that record true as the room changes (the Memory Graph: supersession, audience, routing; state, not log).

People who share physical workspaces in shifts, in fabrication labs, design studios, and research labs, rely on those spaces as rich, structured environments for making and thinking. The workspace is shared and the equipment is shared, but the people are rarely there at the same time. The profile is sequential copresence: the same physical environment, occupied in sequence. Every person who walks in faces state changes and dependencies created by people they cannot see: a swapped nozzle, a re-zeroed scale, a rig that must not be touched until tomorrow. Nothing about the current appearance of the room announces any of this.

The arriver's problem is workspace awareness (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002), the workspace-specific form of situation awareness (Endsley, 1995), in its past tense: knowing what has happened in the space, who did it, when, and what state things are in now. The knowledge exists, in the head of the person who just left, and the room does not carry it.

Today the carrying is done by placeless channels: chat threads, shared docs, whiteboards by the door. These strip the knowledge from the place and moment it belongs to, and, as the cost ledger below shows, they price small observations out entirely, so most of this awareness is never externalized at all. AR annotation systems anchor content to place but treat space as a pin-board for isolated comments: they deliver words, but keep no state.

We introduce Spatial Memory, an interaction paradigm in which the workspace itself carries its past: voice notes spoken in one breath at the thing they concern, anchored where they were said, structured by the Memory Graph (supersession, audience, routing), and surfaced to each later arriver where and when they become relevant. The space becomes a maintained record of its own history that can be navigated by place, by person, or by project.

Why It Goes Unsaid: The Cost Structure

Clark and Brennan (1991) supply the motivation engine. Communication effort is paid against a medium-specific cost structure, and, under the principle of least collaborative effort, people communicate only what the costs make rational. Their cost ledger explains why encounter-class awareness never gets externalized today, and it specifies what a Spatial Memory system has to absorb to change that.

In a co-present, real-time exchange, the effort is shared between two parties and paid as the conversation unfolds. In a space that is shared but occupied in sequence, there is no live partner to share the effort: the whole cost falls on the author, at capture time, for a payoff that goes to someone else, later. When the cost of contributing a small observation exceeds what it appears to be worth to the person paying it, silence is the rational choice. The knowledge is not lost to forgetting or indifference; it is lost to a cost structure that makes communicating it locally irrational.

Channels make this worse in a specific, cited way: in media where changing speaker is "a little like starting up a communication from scratch," people "try to do more within a turn" (pp. 144–145). That composed, effortful channel message is exactly what prices encounter-class information out; a one-breath note spoken in place is the counter-design. Under sequential copresence the eleven costs sort into a division of labor: some are the design brief (drive them toward zero or the note is never spoken), some are erased or destroyed by the profile itself, and some become the evaluation's instrument:

Read as a ledger: the speaker-side and start-up rows are the design brief, the addressee-side rows (understanding, fault, repair) are the evaluation's instrument, and delay and asynchrony belong to neither list: the profile erases the first and destroys the second, and the destroyed one is precisely why the record itself, not any exchange, has to carry the state of the room. The Gap: The Past Half of Workspace Awareness

Gutwin and Greenberg's framework is the reference account of workspace awareness, and it was built for real-time distributed groupware: people working "at the same time, but from different places" (p. 413). But the framework's own element tables reach further than its setting. Alongside the present-tense elements (who is here, what are they doing, where), their second element table enumerates the elements of workspace awareness relating to the past. We call these six questions the arriver's questions, and they anchor everything downstream (the note's fields, the encounter design, the dimension table on the Plan tab):

CategoryElementQuestion it answers (verbatim, Table II, p. 422)
HowAction historyHow did that operation happen?
HowArtifact historyHow did this artifact come to be in this state?
WhenEvent historyWhen did that event happen?
Who (past)Presence historyWho was here, and when?
Where (past)Location historyWhere has a person been?
What (past)Action historyWhat has a person been doing?

These are exactly the questions an arriver in a shift-shared lab is asking. And they are the half of the framework that was never built: when the paper turns to interface techniques, it states "We concentrate here on real-time aspects of workspace awareness – elements that answer the who, what, and where questions" (p. 434). The catalogue that follows (telepointers, radar views, embodiments) works by re-transmitting a live actor across distance, so it has nothing to offer once the actor has left. The thread was picked up once, descriptively: Tam and Greenberg (2006) carried the past elements into a framework of asynchronous change awareness, scoped to documents and digital workspaces. Two decades on, surveys of workspace awareness in XR still organize around the framework and still name the asynchronous case as open (Ens et al., 2019; Assaf et al., 2024). The arriver's questions have never been operationalized as interface techniques for a physically shared, sequentially occupied workspace; that is the slot Spatial Memory fills.

The framework also predicts why the sequential case is hard. G&G identify three channels through which awareness information is gathered: watching people work (consequential communication), artifacts giving off state (feedthrough), and talk and gesture (intentional communication). Under "a spatial or temporal separation between the artifact and the actor," they note, feedthrough is left as "the only vehicle for information" (pp. 423–424). Sequential copresence is the extreme of that observation: the actor is always absent, so of the three channels only feedthrough survives, and raw physical feedthrough shows the present without the past. An artifact's appearance carries no history, no authorship, and no currency: a nudged camera rig looks exactly like a calibrated one, a dryer mid-run looks idle, a re-zeroed scale looks normal.

The setting is unserved in the literature, not empty of occupants. Replay systems stand in it: Asynchronous Reality captures physical events and replays them to a later user, and a long line of AR annotation systems pins notes to places. So does the oldest medium of all: the sticky note on the broken machine is this setting's folk medium, and it has worked for decades. But all of these deliver content without maintaining a picture. A replayed event stream answers "what happened" only by making the arriver watch all of it; a pinned note has no currency, so an "out of PLA" sign outlives its own truth; none of them knows who a note is for, whether it has been superseded, or what state the artifact is in now. Spatial Memory is the sticky note given a memory: state, not log.

The Move: Authored Feedthrough, Maintained State

The design follows from the mechanism argument. If raw feedthrough is the only channel that survives the temporal gap, then the system's job is to author into the artifact the information its appearance does not give off. A note spoken in one breath at the thing answers exactly the arriver's questions the appearance leaves open: artifact history ("swapped the 0.6 nozzle in for the draft job"), event timing ("started the logging run at 4pm"), authorship, and, through the Memory Graph, currency. What transfers from copresence is the referential payoff: the later reader stands where the note was spoken, so an indicative "this one" keeps its referent and the context does not have to be rebuilt from language, the burden Clark and Brennan assign to email readers (p. 143).

Maintenance is the second half of the move. Gutwin and Greenberg's own characterization of awareness (p. 416) is that "environments change over time, so awareness is knowledge that must be maintained and kept up to date." A log cannot do that; a pinned note cannot do that. The Memory Graph's supersession is awareness maintenance made mechanical: when a later note contradicts an earlier one, the record revises itself, and the arriver's picture tracks the room rather than the room's history of announcements. This is the state-not-log principle on the Concept tab, now with its theoretical job description.

One further element goes beyond what any feedthrough could carry. G&G exclude future-directed elements from their framework because, unlike past and present information, belief about the future "involves inference, extrapolation, and prediction" and cannot be determined from raw perception (p. 421). An authored note is not bound by perception: "epoxy cures 24 hours, do not sand before Thursday morning" hands the arriver a dependency that no amount of looking at the part reveals. Directed and routed notes are the system reaching past the perceptual bound that raw artifacts cannot cross.

Read back, the chain is short, and each link hands off to the next: sequential copresence names the setting; the setting severs every awareness channel that needs the actor; the surviving channel, feedthrough, shows the present without the past; the note authors the past back into the place; and the Memory Graph keeps what was authored true as the room keeps changing. Every mechanism on the Concept tab (the two memory types, supersession, audience, routing, the traversals) serves one link of this chain. What the two studies measure, and how they divide the cost ledger above, is on the Plan tab.
