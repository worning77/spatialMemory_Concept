1 · Introduction

People who share physical workspaces at different times of day, in fabrication labs, design studios, and research labs, rely on those spaces as rich environments for making and thinking, yet they communicate with one another through placeless tools: chat channels, shared documents, message threads. Clark and Brennan explain why this arrangement stays quiet. In a co-present, live exchange, the effort of grounding is shared between two parties and paid as the conversation unfolds. In a space that is shared but never occupied at the same moment, there is no partner to share the effort and no exchange to spread it across: the whole cost falls on the author at capture time. To pass on a small observation, the author must switch out of the task, open a channel, name a recipient, and compose in words the context that a gesture at the thing would have carried, all for a payoff that goes to someone else, later, whom the author may never see collect it. When the cost of contributing exceeds what the contribution appears to be worth to the person paying it, silence is the rational choice.

What dies under this cost structure is specific. Encounter-class information, the nozzle is now coarse, the shelf was reorganized, the data stopped logging, is small, situated, and worthless in a channel, yet decisive at the moment a later collaborator encounters the object it concerns. It is not lost to forgetting or indifference; it is lost to a cost structure that makes communicating it locally irrational.

Consider the shared printer. On Monday, Sam swaps a coarse nozzle into the lab's Bambu P2S for a draft run and, without leaving the task, says so in one breath; the note anchors to the printer. The machine already guards its own numbers, temperatures, progress, job state; the note carries the three things the machine cannot say: the intent behind the swap, the permission to swap back, and a pointer to where the fine nozzle sits. On Tuesday, Renata arrives to print fine work. Standing at the printer, she hears the note where it applies, swaps the nozzle back, and speaks a note that supersedes Sam's. Sam chose no recipient and described no referent the place did not already supply; Renata's action and her superseding note complete the exchange, one day late and without either party waiting on the other.

The positioning claim is a single sentence. Clark and Brennan's Table 1 (p. 142) tabulates seven media against eight constraints, and copresence appears exactly once, bundled with cotemporality in face-to-face; every asynchronous medium they analyze is placeless. We extend their media analysis with a row the table does not contain: sequential copresence, copresence without cotemporality, the same physical environment occupied in sequence. What transfers into this cell is copresence's referential payoff: deixis keeps its referent because the reader stands where the note was spoken (p. 145), and context does not have to be imagined from words (p. 143). What does not transfer is every affordance that presupposes a simultaneous partner: timing, live gesture, immediate repair (p. 144). The residue is the ambiguity Clark and Brennan name for email, whether a message was received, read, understood, or ignored (p. 146), and it is the boundary the whole paper is honest about.

The carve follows from the cost analysis. Spatial Memory carries encounter-class information only. Episode-class content, design rationales, weekly plans, decisions that need an audience beyond a place, is composed deliberately, already justifies its channel cost, and stays in the channels and documents that serve it well. We displace nothing that works; we catch what currently falls through.

3.1 The empty cell: sequential copresence

Clark and Brennan characterize a medium by eight constraints it may hold for its participants: copresence, visibility, audibility, cotemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, reviewability, and revisability (p. 141). Their Table 1 (p. 142) tabulates seven media against these constraints, and the table has a hole. Copresence appears exactly once, for face-to-face, where it is bundled with cotemporality; every asynchronous medium they analyze (letters, answering machines, email) is placeless. Same place, different time is a profile the theory can express but never instantiates. We do not extend grounding theory; Clark and Brennan already analyze asynchronous media in depth. We extend their media analysis with a row the table does not contain, and we name the profile sequential copresence: collaborators share the same physical environment, which is exactly how copresence is defined ("A and B share the same physical environment," p. 141), but they occupy it in sequence rather than at once.

The extension is honest about what transfers and what does not. What transfers is copresence's referential payoff: the later reader stands where the note was spoken, so an indicative "this one" keeps its referent (display costs, p. 145), and the context does not have to be imagined from words, which is the burden Clark and Brennan assign to email readers (understanding costs, p. 143). What does not transfer is every affordance that presupposes a simultaneous partner: precise timing, live gesture, monitoring, immediate repair (p. 144). The residue has a name in the theory: "In a medium such as email, B's lack of response can be highly ambiguous. Did she not get the message, did she get it and not read it, did she read it and choose not to respond, did she not understand it, or what?" (p. 146). That state ambiguity, states 0-3 invisible to the author, is the failure the scenario dramatizes and the evaluation tests.

The cell is empty in the theory, not empty of occupants. Replay systems stand in this quadrant, AR annotation pins notes to places, and the sticky note on the broken machine is the quadrant's folk medium. All of them carry delivery, not grounding: a pinned note offers no evidence of understanding, no supersession when it goes stale, no audience, no repair. Spatial Memory is the sticky note given the grounding machinery it never had.

3.2 Three substitutions

Grounding, in Clark and Brennan a synchronous, two-party achievement, is relocated into a space-anchored, accumulated structure through three substitutions. Each converts a move a live partner would otherwise carry into a property of the record, and each carries an old link: the element of the 1991 analysis it substitutes for.

Grounding moveSubstitutionOld link (C&B 1991)
choose a recipientanchor to a place: the note attaches to the place, not a named addresseestart-up and speaker-change costs are what price out addressing (pp. 143-145)
describe the referentspatial co-location: the reader later stands where it was said, so deixis is restored across timecontext is present, not imagined from words (p. 143); indicative reference regains its referent (p. 145)
accept with a live replydeferred acceptance: a later person's action on the world stands in for the replythe acceptance phase (p. 130); async states 0-3 are invisible to the author (p. 146)

The third substitution is load-bearing and fragile. A deferred action is not the mutual belief a live reply secures: asynchrony removes repair, so grounding can complete only one-way, and we report where it lands rather than claiming it closes. This boundary is not hidden in the design; it is what the evaluation instruments.

3.3 The loop and the four properties

Spatial Memory closes a four-step loop. A worker captures a judgment by speaking one sentence in place, at low effort; the system anchors it to the place where it was spoken; the Memory Graph structures it, assigning type, audience, urgency, and any supersession; and when the next person approaches, the place surfaces its current, relevant state. The loop runs the substitutions directly: anchor performs the recipient substitution, surface performs the co-location substitution, and a later person's action on what surfaces performs the acceptance substitution. No single step is new; the contribution is closing the loop for situated judgments in shared physical work.

Four properties follow, each with its old link. Anchored: notes attach to the place spoken; the recipient substitution made physical (start-up cost near zero, pp. 143-145). Accumulated: the room builds up over time, from many people and projects; reviewability (p. 141) made spatial, and what makes deferred acceptance legible as a trace. Layered: notes differ along audience and urgency, two orthogonal axes, who may see versus when and how loudly; urgency has exactly two levels, info and warning, and nothing blocks. Navigable: the record is versioned, filterable, and traversable, not a pile of pins; the reader's side of grounding under accumulation, mechanized by the Memory Graph below.

Low effort, not no effort. Every note is a deliberate utterance: no ambient inference, no gaze or dwell logging, no telemetry. The system removes only the addressing effort, never authorship, which is also the privacy stance: capture of chosen speech, not surveillance.

3.4 The information it serves

Spatial Memory does not replace the channels that already work; it catches what those channels price out. Encounter-class information has low value to any single author, high cumulative value to the group, a spatial referent, and no known reader when spoken. Its grounding cost, paid alone for someone else's later benefit, exceeds its apparent worth, so today it goes unsaid. Episode-class messages have a known recipient and bounded content; a channel tool is already the right medium, and Spatial Memory never sees them. This carve is the claim boundary, and the scenario stages its violation: the week's only directed note is an encounter-class observation misaddressed as an episode.

Every note is one of two memory types, defined by two questions: what it is anchored to, and what it outlives.

TypeAnchored toOutlivesExample
Object memorya placeany project (institutional)"0.6 nozzle is in" on the printer

Person and time are not types but queries over the graph: whose trail, addressed to whom, since when. A note leaves the record by supersession, by named archive (the creator, a directed recipient, or a resolver leaving their name), or by persistence expiry; there is no bare delete.

3.6 The cost ledger

Clark and Brennan price grounding through eleven costs that shift with the medium (pp. 142-145). Under sequential copresence the whole cost of a contribution falls on the author at capture time, for a payoff that goes to someone else, later; when that cost exceeds the note's apparent worth, silence is rational, and the knowledge is lost to a cost structure, not to forgetting. The eleven costs therefore sort into a division of labor: some are the design brief, some become the evaluation's instrument, and some are erased or destroyed by the profile itself. 4 Scenario

The scenario is one week in a shared maker lab, told as continuous narrative. It does double duty: it is the coverage proof that every mechanism the Concept section claims actually earns its place in a plausible week of work, and it is the design spec for the Study 2 seed set, because every beat maps to an experimental item. The paper's genre demands this discipline: a concept proposal cannot let its scenario be decorative.

Setting and cast

SettingA shared maker lab: one Bambu P2S printer, a hand-capture rig (a cube frame over the capture bench, one camera at each of its four corners, markerless EasyMocap-style), a shared device shelf, a soldering station, five workstations. Projects share the fixtures; the people rarely overlap in time.

The week

Monday. Sam prepares the Project A capture. He swaps in a 0.6 hardened nozzle and carbon-fiber filament, sets the printer menu and slicer to match, and leaves a note at the printer: the swap is deliberate, swapping back is allowed, the 0.4 is in the tray under the bench. The machine guards its own numbers; only the note carries intent, permission, and a pointer. He files one thought to the project rather than a place: shoot the A capture at 120fps, not the default 60. He recalibrates the rig, which afterwards looks exactly as it did yesterday, and notes it: "Recalibrated the rig this morning." He clears a stray personal box to the shelf with a note at the empty spot, takes the shared watch with a note at its shelf slot, and leaves, offsite until Thursday: four place-anchored broadcasts, one project note, no recipients named.

Tuesday. Renata arrives to print a fine plate; Sam's note is waiting at the machine. She swaps back to 0.4 and PLA and her sentence stacks on top of his: the printer now shows what is true, history folded beneath. Priya finds the watch's shelf slot empty but answered; she replies into the thread, "Need it Thursday 10am, leaving it on the shelf works." Dae-ho follows the bench-spot note to his box and archives the note as handled; carrying the box, he clips one of the rig's corner cameras. Nothing looks different, but the rig is plainly Sam's setup, so he addresses the owner: "Dae-ho to Sam: clipped one of the rig cameras, looks fine, but you'd know better." Encounter-class information, misaddressed as episode-class. Sam is away until Thursday; the card waits.

Wednesday. Iris, new to Project A, asks for Sam's trail and walks his Monday in order, collecting the mount on the way. At the rig, the 120fps project card surfaces, spoken at the printer, routed to the member doing the work it describes; she sets the frame rate. She reads Sam's calibration note, sees nothing that says anyone has touched the rig, and taps it confirmed. She captures all afternoon, four live views, no warnings, and files a completion report. Four green lights: delivered, read, confirmed with her name on the checkmark, and a relevant next turn. Dae-ho's card sits anchored to this same rig, ten feet away, invisible to her by its author's addressing choice. Her data is silently off.

Thursday. Sam returns and asks the room the arrival question; triage hands him three cards, and the two that spent Wednesday ten feet apart now sit side by side. He drops the watch on its shelf slot and Priya's request closes without another word: deferred acceptance, clean. At the rig, the camera Dae-ho named sits a degree shy of Monday's calibration; he re-runs the board, finds every take skewed, and Iris re-captures on the fresh calibration. The repair lands two days after the fault. Sam freezes the lesson into structure: "Before any capture, re-run the board," confirmed as a Depends_On rule that surfaces before every capture note. He archives Dae-ho's card as handled.

Friday. Priya leaves for the weekend talking in one breath: the watch drains fast when logging; the soldering station's USB-C is fraying. The room splits the breath into two cards, an info note at the shelf slot and a warning at the station; at the door she looks back at where they landed and signs the placement with a yes. After lunch the warning reaches Dae-ho ahead of everything at the station, ahead even of the standing tips note; he swaps the cable and his sentence supersedes the warning within four hours, against the two days his own directed card waited. Renata prints one more plate; the printer shows one card, hers, still true, and the nozzle question never enters her mind.

5 · Implementation

The system section is an instantiation, not a load-bearing engineering claim: it shows that the loop the Concept section argues for can actually run on hardware people can wear today. Its technical core mirrors AsyncReality's Causality Graph. Where Fender and Holz's graph makes replayed events causally coherent for a later viewer, the Memory Graph makes accumulated utterances groundable for a later reader; and where their graph is written by event capture, ours is written by a structuring pipeline that turns one breath of speech into typed, linked, placed, signed cards. The graph governs surfacing; the pipeline is its writer; the LLM appears only inside the pipeline, as a proposer whose every output a human confirms.

5.1 Platform and anchors

The prototype runs on Meta Quest 3 in passthrough, built in Unity with the Meta XR SDK, as a functional proxy for lightweight AR glasses: the target form factor has position tracking and audio I/O but no outward-facing task cameras, and AsyncReality established the same proxy precedent. Shared spatial anchors persist across sessions and users, so a card placed on Monday is world-locked in the same spot on Wednesday for a different wearer. Places are represented as named zones, volumes bound to anchors and labeled once at setup (printer, capture bench, device shelf, soldering station); a note anchors to the zone the speaker stands in, refined by where they look. The system is SLAM-only by design: no object recognition, no scene understanding. The camera is a locator, not an observer. Objects enter the record by being named in the utterance (the P2S, the watch, camera three of the rig), which keeps the sensing burden minimal and the privacy posture defensible.

5.2 Capture

Capture is the headset microphone plus cloud speech-to-text. The interaction is hold-to-speak: press, say the note while continuing to work, release. The design target is the one-breath discipline from the scenario: Priya's single Friday utterance about the shelf and the soldering iron is the intended unit of input, one uninterrupted spoken span, produced without leaving the task. Everything downstream exists so that this one breath is enough; the speaker never types, files, or addresses anything by hand unless they choose to override a proposal.

5.3 The structuring pipeline

The pipeline is where the LLM lives, and it lives there strictly as a proposer. It performs three proposal steps over the transcript. Segmentation: one breath may contain more than one note; Priya's utterance becomes two proposed cards, a shelf restock info card and a soldering-station warning card, each with its own anchor. Metadata proposal: for each segment the LLM proposes an anchor zone (from the speaker's position, gaze, and named referents), an audience (broadcast, project, or directed), and an urgency level (info or warning). Link inference: correction language ("actually," "swapped back," "ignore the earlier note") proposes a Supersedes edge; prerequisite language ("before you print," "read this first") proposes Depends_On; reference language ("about Sam's note," "re the watch") proposes Replies_To.

None of these proposals takes effect on its own. Every card and every link is shown back to the speaker for confirmation, and nothing enters the shared record unsigned: the confirmed card carries its author's name as a signature, which is what later makes a named archive and a Confirmed_by acknowledgment meaningful acts by identifiable people. Anchor confirmation uses a look-back placement confirm: the proposed card renders in place at its proposed anchor, the speaker glances at it, and a single tap accepts, or a drag relocates it. The Friday beat depends on exactly this step; Priya's look-back is the moment the warning becomes anchored where Dae-ho will walk four hours later.

5.4 Graph store and surfacing

The store is a directed typed graph, synchronized across users through a cloud backend and summarized below without formalism.

NodesNote cards (signed, transcribed, anchored); zones; people (as authors, addressees, confirmers); projects. Two memory types only, object and project, defined by what a note is anchored to and what it outlives; person and time are queries over the graph, not node types.
Structural edgesAnchored_to (note to zone), left_by (note to person), directed_to (note to person, the routing case), part_of (note to project).
Semantic edgesSupersedes (correction, version currency), Depends_On (prerequisite ordering, read-first), Replies_To (threading), Confirmed_by (acknowledgment, makes reception visible).
AttributesAudience (who may see) and urgency (when and how loudly), orthogonal axes. Urgency has exactly two levels, info and warning; there is no blocking level anywhere in the system.
SalienceSurfacing order combines recency, urgency, dependency ordering (a Depends_On target surfaces before its dependent), and version resolution (a superseded note is hidden behind its successor).
LifecycleA note leaves default surfacing by supersession, by named archive (the creator, the directed recipient, or a resolver who leaves their name), or by persistence expiry. There is no bare delete; the record is append-mostly and every removal has an author.

Surfacing is query execution. The five traversals are the complete retrieval vocabulary:

notes_atAll visible, active notes anchored to the current zone, salience-ordered. The default encounter mode; runs continuously as the wearer moves.
trailOne person's notes in temporal order with their anchors; walked physically. Iris's Wednesday onboarding.
threadThe closure of a note over Replies_To and Supersedes edges; a conversation reconstructed across places and days. Priya's watch negotiation.
recentEverything visible since the wearer's last visit; the arrival summary.
for_meActive notes with a directed_to edge to the wearer; triage on arrival. How Sam's Thursday pool assembles.

5.5 Interaction techniques as implementation choices

Several presentation decisions are implementation choices of this prototype, explicitly not part of the concept, and other instantiations could choose differently. A zone with one note shows a full card; two to five notes stack by recency; beyond five the stack collapses to a count badge that expands on demand. Superseded notes sit behind their successor in a version history the reader can unfold, which is how Iris can see both Renata's swap and Sam's original if she wants the story. A scope toggle switches between immediate scope (recent notes, daily orientation) and historical scope (the full accumulated record). These thresholds and toggles tune density; the claims of the paper do not rest on them.

5.6 Study apparatus

Two apparatus modes support the evaluation. For Study 1, capture is wizarded: audio and location are genuinely recorded while the leaver works, but no live pipeline runs behind the interaction; the recordings later feed the offline segmentation benchmark that constitutes the technical evaluation. For Study 2, the system runs for real over a pre-populated graph: the frozen seed set, curated from Study 1 utterances plus researcher fillers, is loaded identically into two matched rooms of roughly 35 to 40 notes, including superseded pairs and notes addressed to other people, so that the SM-full and anchored-only conditions differ in structure alone. The seed loader and the ablation switch (graph reasoning off, raw anchored cards only) are part of the system build.

6Evaluation

A synchronous grounding experiment, from Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs onward, holds speaker and addressee in a single session: the same pair, the same referents, turn after turn, so that grounding can be watched as it happens. Sequential copresence separates the two sides by definition; no session contains both people, and any design that puts them in one room has stopped testing the medium. The evaluation therefore does not approximate the standard experiment; it cuts it at the asynchrony line and tests each half where it lives. Study 1 tests the speaker half: when the author-side costs are removed (choosing a recipient, formulating for an absent addressee, transmitting), does encounter-class information that a placeless channel prices out actually get released, measured as externalization rate against a Slack baseline. The frozen seed set is the turn passed across the cut: real Study 1 utterances, structured by the real pipeline, curated once, frozen, and installed identically for every arriver. Study 2 tests the addressee half: does understanding survive when no live partner is present to repair it. RQ3, the segmentation benchmark, sits at the joint between the halves and is the technical evaluation of the tech core.

Methods lineage

The measurement vocabulary is taken from the grounding tradition rather than from usability testing. Every trap is a referential task in the Clark and Wilkes-Gibbs lineage: resolve a referent, then act on it. The condition contrast follows Gergle's constraint-ablation discipline: removing one constraint, here the structure layer, pre-declares which indicators should move and which should stand still, before any data are collected, and the task set carries graded grounding-demand items so the dissociation has room to show. Scoring follows Monk and Watts in coding conduct, not just outcome: people compensate, so outcome alone is insensitive, and the compensation behaviors themselves are predictions. Detection follows Yamashita: misreadings are located behaviorally in the action log, because blocked repair channels produce silent commitment rather than flagged confusion; and ambiguity is situational, arising from a world that changed after the leaver departed, never from artificially vague wording. The seed design follows Dillenbourg and Traum's fact-versus-inference boundary: persistence substitutes for perception-level grounding of facts but not for agreement on inferences, so fact notes are controls and inference notes are traps. Trap construction follows Convertino's hidden-profile logic: every trap note is a one-sided con set, true but incomplete, with the completing fact staged in the room, and trap severity is pilot-calibrated toward the 36 to 46 percent success band so no trap floors or ceilings. Finally, the solo arriver is justified in the method's own vocabulary: a solo arriver is structurally a homogeneous group, the configuration in which hidden-profile pooling fails hardest, and the room plays the second role; a live partner would smuggle cotemporality back into the medium under test.

Study 1: the Leaver (speaker half)

N ≈ 10, within-subject, roughly 40 minutes per session, in-lab on Quest 3 in passthrough. The cover story casts the participant as the morning shift, handed over that afternoon to someone they do not know; recording is never mentioned, so externalizing stays optional and costly, as in life. Both conditions wear the headset, holding device burden constant; the only variable is the capture channel: speak in place (wizarded, audio and location genuinely recorded, no live pipeline) versus phone in pocket with lab Slack channels available. The 30-minute task plants six encounters, three caused by the participant and three discovered, three of which feed the Study 2 traps. A post-task manipulation-check interview separates did-not-notice from noticed-but-did-not-record; only the latter is the phenomenon. Measures: externalization rate per noticed encounter, time-off-task per record, NASA-TLX, blinded usefulness rating, and an exit novelty check.

RQ3: the segmentation benchmark (technical evaluation of the tech core)

The tech core is the Memory Graph plus the structuring pipeline that writes it: speech becomes typed, linked, placed, signed cards, with the LLM appearing only as the proposer inside the structure step. The benchmark runs that pipeline offline over the full Study 1 corpus plus pilot recordings, against gold labels, and reports: segmentation boundary accuracy; object-versus-project filing; anchor proposal accuracy; audience and urgency proposal (info versus warning); link inference for supersession and replies; and capture-to-card latency. Because nothing enters the shared record unsigned, proposal accuracy is also a burden measure: we report the correction rate per card, the edits a human must make before signing. A separate anchoring benchmark runs repeated approach trials across sessions and relocalizations on Quest 3, reporting surfacing reliability and latency. The graph governs surfacing; the benchmark establishes that the pipeline can feed the graph from real working speech.

Study 2: the Arriver (addressee half)

N = 12 to 16, within-subject, roughly 50 minutes per session, Quest 3, cast as the afternoon shift, new to the project. Two matched pre-seeded rooms, counterbalanced: SM-full (graph on: triage, supersession, audience filter, trail) versus anchored-only (the ablation: the same 35 to 40 cards raw in place, the stale card hanging beside its successor, no filter, no triage). Each trap is scored on the ladder: skipped, referent unresolved, referent found but intent misread, grounded; the last two rungs are separated by paraphrase probes ("what did the author want you to do, and why"), never by asking "did you understand". 7 · Discussion (+ Related Work deltas)

The boundary verdict

The first two substitutions operate on cost, a property of the medium the design controls. The third is different in kind: deferred acceptance replaces a live reply with a later person's action on the world, and whether that action closes grounding depends on whether the actor understood the original intent, which no design can guarantee. Clark and Brennan name the failure precisely: in email, a sender cannot distinguish states 0 through 3, whether the message arrived, was seen, was read, or was understood (p.146). The Memory Graph's evidence mechanisms recover most of this ladder: Iris's four green lights (delivered, read, confirmed, completion report) return to Sam exactly the state information the p.146 ambiguity says the medium withholds. What they cannot recover is the timing of repair. Iris confirms the calibration note and still captures with the data feed silently off; the repair arrives Thursday, two days late, as a pooled triage rather than a next turn. The evaluation reports this honestly: the rate at which deferred acceptance lands grounded versus misread, and the failure modes a live repair turn would have caught. Whether that residue means deferred acceptance is a weaker form of grounding or the point where the theory's scope ends is the verdict the studies deliver; either answer is a contribution, because the theory characterizes this profile without ever instantiating it.

The unwitting author

Capture is deliberate utterance: nothing enters the shared record unsigned, and nothing is recorded that was not spoken to the system. The price of this stance is the unwitting author. A person who changes the world without knowing it, who nudges a fixture or borrows a cable and thinks nothing of it, writes nothing, and the room stays silent about the change. This is a stated boundary of the concept, not a flaw of the system: Spatial Memory covers what people know they know and are willing to say; ambient change detection belongs to a different, surveillance-adjacent design space we decline.

Room versus whiteboard

Dillenbourg and Traum showed grounding migrating onto a persistent whiteboard: one task, one surface, one hour. A multi-project room across weeks changes the economics. Persistence alone yields accumulation without relevance; every visitor would face everyone's residue. This is why the medium needs the graph: Triage's purpose-relative salience, Audience scoping, and Supersedes pruning are what make persistence survive scale. The whiteboard result predicts fact notes travel well and inference notes travel poorly, which is exactly the fact-as-control, inference-as-trap prediction Study 2 tests.

Process common ground accrues

Convertino et al. distinguish content common ground from process common ground, knowledge of how the group works. Thursday's lesson, frozen as a Depends_On read-first rule on the rig, is process common ground written into space: the team's hard-won procedure becomes a structural property of the place that binds arrivers who never met the people who learned it.

The addressing default

The week argues for a default: address the place, not the person. Dae-ho's only directed note misaddresses an encounter-class fact as an episode-class message and waits two days; Priya's broadcast warning at the soldering station reaches him in four hours. Directed notes remain correct for genuinely episode-class content; the design's position is that the system suggests the addressing, via Routing and audience proposal, and the human decides at signing.

Privacy stance, recapped

No passive capture, no ambient recording, no bare delete: every removal carries a name. Every card is an authored, signed utterance; the camera serves as locator, not recorder; trail visibility defaults to direct collaborators with broader access opt-in. The lifecycle rule that archiving requires a name (creator, directed recipient, or resolver) keeps accountability symmetric with authorship.
