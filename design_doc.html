1 · Introduction

People who share physical workspaces in shifts, in fabrication labs, design studios, and research labs, rely on those spaces as rich environments for making and thinking, but they are rarely in them at the same time. Every person who walks in faces state changes and dependencies created by people they cannot see: a swapped nozzle, a re-zeroed scale, a rig that must not be touched until tomorrow, and nothing about the room's current appearance announces any of it. The arriver's problem is workspace awareness, the workspace-specific form of situation awareness, in its past tense: what happened here, who did it, when, and what state things are in now. The knowledge exists, in the head of the person who just left; the room does not carry it.

Today the carrying is done by placeless tools: chat channels, shared documents, message threads. Clark and Brennan explain why this arrangement stays quiet. In a co-present, live exchange, the effort of communicating is shared between two parties and paid as the conversation unfolds. In a space that is shared but never occupied at the same moment, there is no partner to share the effort and no exchange to spread it across: the whole cost falls on the author at capture time. To pass on a small observation, the author must switch out of the task, open a channel, name a recipient, and compose in words the context that a gesture at the thing would have carried, all for a payoff that goes to someone else, later, whom the author may never see collect it. When the cost of contributing exceeds what the contribution appears to be worth to the person paying it, silence is the rational choice.

What dies under this cost structure is specific. Encounter-class information, the nozzle is now coarse, the shelf was reorganized, the data stopped logging, is small, situated, and worthless in a channel, yet decisive at the moment a later collaborator encounters the object it concerns. It is not lost to forgetting or indifference; it is lost to a cost structure that makes communicating it locally irrational.

Consider the shared printer. On Monday, Sam swaps a coarse nozzle into the lab's Bambu P2S for a draft run and, without leaving the task, says so in one breath; the note anchors to the printer. The machine already guards its own numbers, temperatures, progress, job state; the note carries the three things the machine cannot say: the intent behind the swap, the permission to swap back, and a pointer to where the fine nozzle sits. On Tuesday, Renata arrives to print fine work. Standing at the printer, she hears the note where it applies, swaps the nozzle back, and speaks a note that supersedes Sam's. Sam chose no recipient and described no referent the place did not already supply; Renata's action and her superseding note complete the exchange, one day late and without either party waiting on the other.

The positioning claim is a single sentence. The reference account of workspace awareness was built for real-time distributed groupware, people working "at the same time, but from different places" (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002, p. 413); its own second element table names the awareness of the past, exactly the arriver's questions, how did this artifact come to be in this state, when did that happen, who was here (Table II, p. 422), yet its technique catalogue serves only the present tense (p. 434), the thread was carried on only descriptively and only for digital documents (Tam & Greenberg, 2006), and XR surveys still name the asynchronous case open (Ens et al., 2019; Assaf et al., 2024). The framework even predicts why the sequential case is hard: of the three channels through which awareness information is gathered, watching people work, artifacts giving off state, and talk, only feedthrough survives "a spatial or temporal separation between the artifact and the actor" (pp. 423–424), and raw feedthrough shows the present without the past: a nudged rig looks calibrated. We call the setting sequential copresence, the same physical environment occupied in sequence, and we answer it by authoring the past into the place: one-breath notes carry the how, when, and who that appearances lack, and the Memory Graph keeps the record true as the room keeps changing, awareness "maintained and kept up to date" made mechanical (p. 416).

The carve follows from the cost analysis. Spatial Memory carries encounter-class information only. Episode-class content, design rationales, weekly plans, decisions that need an audience beyond a place, is composed deliberately, already justifies its channel cost, and stays in the channels and documents that serve it well. We displace nothing that works; we catch what currently falls through.

3.1 The setting: sequential copresence and the arriver's questions

People who share physical workspaces in shifts, in fabrication labs, design studios, and research labs, rely on those spaces as rich, structured environments for making and thinking. The workspace is shared and the equipment is shared, but the people are rarely there at the same time. The profile is sequential copresence: the same physical environment, occupied in sequence. Every person who walks in faces state changes and dependencies created by people they cannot see: a swapped nozzle, a re-zeroed scale, a rig that must not be touched until tomorrow. Nothing about the current appearance of the room announces any of this.

The arriver's problem is workspace awareness (Gutwin & Greenberg, 2002), the workspace-specific form of situation awareness (Endsley, 1995), in its past tense: knowing what has happened in the space, who did it, when, and what state things are in now. Gutwin and Greenberg's framework is the reference account, and it was built for real-time distributed groupware: people working "at the same time, but from different places" (p. 413). But the framework's own element tables reach further than its setting. Alongside the present-tense elements (who is here, what are they doing, where), their second element table enumerates the elements of workspace awareness relating to the past. We call these six questions the arriver's questions, and they anchor everything downstream: the note's fields, the encounter design, the dimension table on the Plan tab.

CategoryElementQuestion it answers (verbatim, Table II, p. 422)
HowAction historyHow did that operation happen?
HowArtifact historyHow did this artifact come to be in this state?
WhenEvent historyWhen did that event happen?
Who (past)Presence historyWho was here, and when?
Where (past)Location historyWhere has a person been?
What (past)Action historyWhat has a person been doing?

These are exactly the questions an arriver in a shift-shared lab is asking, and they are the half of the framework that was never built. When the paper turns to interface techniques, it states "We concentrate here on real-time aspects of workspace awareness – elements that answer the who, what, and where questions" (p. 434). The catalogue that follows (telepointers, radar views, embodiments) works by re-transmitting a live actor across distance, so it has nothing to offer once the actor has left. The thread was picked up once, descriptively: Tam and Greenberg (2006) carried the past elements into a framework of asynchronous change awareness, scoped to documents and digital workspaces. Two decades on, surveys of workspace awareness in XR still organize around the framework and still name the asynchronous case as open (Ens et al., 2019; Assaf et al., 2024). The arriver's questions have never been operationalized as interface techniques for a physically shared, sequentially occupied workspace; that is the slot Spatial Memory fills.

The framework also predicts why the sequential case is hard. Gutwin and Greenberg identify three channels through which awareness information is gathered: watching people work (consequential communication), artifacts giving off state (feedthrough), and talk and gesture (intentional communication). Under "a spatial or temporal separation between the artifact and the actor," they note, feedthrough is left as "the only vehicle for information" (pp. 423–424). Sequential copresence is the extreme of that observation: the actor is always absent, so of the three channels only feedthrough survives, and raw physical feedthrough shows the present without the past. An artifact's appearance carries no history, no authorship, and no currency: a nudged camera rig looks exactly like a calibrated one, a dryer mid-run looks idle, a re-zeroed scale looks normal.

The setting is unserved in the literature, not empty of occupants. Replay systems stand in it: Asynchronous Reality captures physical events and replays them to a later user, and a long line of AR annotation systems pins notes to places. So does the oldest medium of all: the sticky note on the broken machine is this setting's folk medium, and it has worked for decades. But all of these deliver content without maintaining a picture. A replayed event stream answers "what happened" only by making the arriver watch all of it; a pinned note has no currency, so an "out of PLA" sign outlives its own truth; none of them knows who a note is for, whether it has been superseded, or what state the artifact is in now. Spatial Memory is the sticky note given a memory: state, not log. Why the one person who could fill the gap stays silent today is a cost question, and Clark and Brennan supply that ledger in Section 3.6.

3.2 The graft: authored feedthrough

The loop's theoretical job is a graft. A note's content is intentional communication: deliberate, authored, able to carry intent and dependencies that no artifact's appearance reveals. Its delivery is feedthrough: anchored in the place, it persists after the actor leaves and reaches whoever walks up next, on the only channel that survives the temporal gap. A note is authored feedthrough, and each loop step serves one piece of the graft:

Loop stepTheory jobTheory link
Capturedrive the author-side costs to zero at the moment of encounterthe cost ledger's design brief: formulation, production, start-up
Anchorgraft the utterance onto the channel that survives the actor's absencefeedthrough: the note persists in the place, like a state the artifact gives off
Structurekeep the record true as the room changesawareness maintenance (G&G, p. 416); the Memory Graph, Section 3.5
Surfaceanswer the arriver's questions at the thing, at the moment they matterhow did this come to be in this state, when, who (the past elements)

Two design ideas do the anchoring work. The note attaches to a place, not a named addressee, so the system removes the addressing effort entirely; and spatial co-location restores deixis across time: the later reader stands where the note was spoken, so an indicative "this one" keeps its referent (p. 145), and the context does not have to be rebuilt from language, the burden Clark and Brennan assign to email readers (p. 143).

One further element goes beyond what any feedthrough could carry. Gutwin and Greenberg exclude future-directed elements from their framework because, unlike past and present information, belief about the future "involves inference, extrapolation, and prediction" and cannot be determined from raw perception (p. 421). An authored note is not bound by perception: "epoxy cures 24 hours, do not sand before Thursday morning" hands the arriver a dependency that no amount of looking at the part reveals. Directed and routed notes are the system reaching past the perceptual bound that raw artifacts cannot cross. Where the arriver's picture can still fail with no one there to ask is not hidden in the design; it is what the cost ledger assigns to the evaluation (Section 3.6).

3.3 The loop and the four properties

Spatial Memory closes a four-step loop. A worker captures a judgment by speaking one sentence in place, at low effort; the system anchors it to the place where it was spoken; the Memory Graph structures it, assigning type, audience, urgency, and any supersession; and when the next person approaches, the place surfaces its current, relevant state. The loop runs the graft directly: capture drives the author-side costs toward zero, anchor grafts the utterance onto feedthrough, structure keeps the record true as the room changes, and surface answers the arriver's questions at the thing. No single step is new; the contribution is closing the loop for situated judgments in shared physical work.

Four properties follow, each serving one link of the chain. Anchored: notes attach to the place spoken; the place is the feedthrough carrier, what the arriver walks up to, and addressing costs nothing (start-up cost near zero, pp. 143-145). Accumulated: the room builds up over time, from many people and projects; how, when, and who remain answerable behind every current state. Layered: notes differ along audience and urgency, two orthogonal axes, who may see versus when and how loudly; urgency has exactly two levels, info and warning, and nothing blocks. Navigable: the record is versioned, filterable, and traversable, not a pile of pins; the arriver's picture kept small and current, mechanized by the Memory Graph below.

Low effort, not no effort. Every note is a deliberate utterance: no ambient inference, no gaze or dwell logging, no telemetry. This is also the line between authoring and capture-and-replay: an authored record can carry intent and dependencies no sensor recovers, and it contains only what someone chose to say. The system removes only the addressing effort, recipient, referent, and app switch, never authorship. That is the privacy stance: capture of chosen speech, not surveillance.

3.4 The information it serves

Spatial Memory does not replace the channels that already work; it catches what those channels price out. Encounter-class information has low value to any single author, high cumulative value to the group, a spatial referent, and no known reader when spoken. Its externalization cost, paid alone for someone else's later benefit, exceeds its apparent worth, so today it goes unsaid. Episode-class messages have a known recipient and bounded content; a channel tool is already the right medium, and Spatial Memory never sees them. This carve is the claim boundary, and the scenario stages its violation: the week's only directed note is an encounter-class observation misaddressed as an episode.

Every note is one of two memory types, defined by two questions: what it is anchored to, and what it outlives.

TypeAnchored toOutlivesExample
Object memorya placeany project (institutional)"0.6 nozzle is in" on the printer
Project memorya project, spanning placesnothing; dies with the project"set the A-capture cameras to 40" follows the project to the rig

Person and time are not types but queries over the graph, presence and action history asked on demand: whose trail, addressed to whom, since when. A note leaves the record by supersession, by named archive (the creator, a directed recipient, or a resolver leaving their name), or by persistence expiry; there is no bare delete.

3.5 The Memory Graph: awareness maintenance made mechanical

The structuring step is a small typed store, but read against Section 3.1 it is not a data model at all: it is awareness maintenance made mechanical. Gutwin and Greenberg's own characterization of awareness is that "environments change over time, so awareness is knowledge that must be maintained and kept up to date" (p. 416); the graph's job is to keep each arriver's picture of the room current, correct, and scoped to them. Each mechanism carries a theory link.

MechanismJobTheory link
Supersedesstate, not log: a place shows what is true nowcurrency: awareness "must be maintained and kept up to date" (G&G, p. 416); the record tracks the room, not the room's history of announcements
Depends_Onprerequisites surface first, "read this first"read-before-act coupling; a dependency is future-directed information no perception recovers (G&G, p. 421)
Replies_Tothreads that span days and peopleevent and action history: how the current state came to be stays answerable behind it
Confirmed_byone-tap "seen, will comply"reverse awareness, for the leaver: the author's picture of whether the note landed; the one move where the arriver speaks back into the record
Audiencebroadcast, directed, or project; each arriver hears only what is for themthe readers dimension: one known reader, whoever comes next, or everyone
Routinga project memory follows the work to its next placespatial scope: awareness that must arrive somewhere other than where it was spoken
Triage + salience"anything for me?" returns a few ordered items; the rest stays quieta picture small enough to act on: warnings first, relevance next, silence otherwise

Read as maintenance jobs: currency (supersedes), ordering (depends_on), history (replies_to, accumulation), scope (audience, routing, triage), and one return channel (confirmed_by). The value is the graph's position in the loop, not the data model, which a plain table could hold. The hard part is building it from messy in-flow speech. The graph governs all surfacing; the structuring pipeline that writes it, where an LLM proposes and a human signs, belongs to the System section.

3.6 The cost ledger

Clark and Brennan supply the motivation engine. Communication effort is paid against a medium-specific cost structure of eleven costs that shift with the medium (pp. 142-145), and people communicate only what the costs make rational: the ledger explains why encounter-class awareness never gets externalized today, and it specifies what the system must absorb to change that. Under sequential copresence the whole cost of a contribution falls on the author at capture time, for a payoff that goes to someone else, later; when that cost exceeds the note's apparent worth, silence is rational, and the knowledge is lost to a cost structure, not to forgetting. Channels sharpen the point: in media where changing speaker is "a little like starting up a communication from scratch," people "try to do more within a turn" (pp. 144-145), and that composed, effortful message is exactly what prices encounter-class information out. The eleven costs therefore sort into a division of labor: some are the design brief, some become the evaluation's instrument, and some are erased or destroyed by the profile itself. The full per-cost table is canonical on the Framing tab; the ledger condenses as follows.

RoleCostsConsequence

Read as a ledger, delay and asynchrony belong to no design list: the profile erases the first and destroys the second, and the destroyed one is precisely why the record itself, not any exchange, has to carry the state of the room. That job goes to the Memory Graph (Section 3.5); where the record still lets the arriver's picture fail is the boundary the scenario dramatizes, the evaluation measures, and the discussion owns. How the two studies divide the ledger is on the Plan tab.

4 Scenario

The scenario is one week in a shared maker lab, told as continuous narrative. It does double duty: it is the coverage proof that every mechanism the Concept section claims actually earns its place in a plausible week of work, and it is the design spec for the Study 2 seed set, because every beat maps to an experimental item. The paper's genre demands this discipline: a concept proposal cannot let its scenario be decorative.

Setting and cast

SettingA shared maker lab: one Bambu P2S printer, a hand-capture rig (a cube frame over the capture bench, one camera at each of its four corners, markerless EasyMocap-style), a shared device shelf, a soldering station, five workstations. Projects share the fixtures; the people rarely overlap in time.
SamProject A. Seeds the room Monday, offsite until Thursday. The week's central author and its central victim.
RenataProject B. Prints Tuesday and Friday; touches only the printer.
PriyaProject D. Needs the shared watch; speaks the week's one segmented breath.
Dae-ho, IrisDae-ho (Project C) retrieves his box, clips a rig camera, leaves the week's only directed note. Iris, new to Project A, runs the capture Wednesday in Sam's absence.

The week

Monday. Sam prepares the Project A capture. He swaps in a 0.6 hardened nozzle and carbon-fiber filament, sets the printer menu and slicer to match, and leaves a note at the printer: the swap is deliberate, swapping back is allowed, the 0.4 is in the tray under the bench. The machine guards its own numbers; only the note carries intent, permission, and a pointer. He files one thought to the project rather than a place: set the A-capture cameras to 40, they come up at 60. He recalibrates the rig, which afterwards looks exactly as it did yesterday, and notes it: "Recalibrated the rig this morning." He clears a stray personal box to the shelf with a note at the empty spot, takes the shared watch with a note at its shelf slot, and leaves, offsite until Thursday: four place-anchored broadcasts, one project note, no recipients named.

Tuesday. Renata arrives to print a fine plate; Sam's note is waiting at the machine, answering the arriver's question before she can ask it: how did this come to be in this state. She swaps back to 0.4 and PLA and her sentence stacks on top of his: the printer now shows what is true, history folded beneath. Priya finds the watch's shelf slot empty but answered; she replies into the thread, "Need it Thursday 10am, leaving it on the shelf works." Dae-ho follows the bench-spot note to his box and archives the note as handled; carrying the box, he clips one of the rig's corner cameras. Nothing looks different, but the rig is plainly Sam's setup, so he addresses the owner: "Dae-ho to Sam: clipped one of the rig cameras, looks fine, but you'd know better." Encounter-class information, misaddressed as episode-class. Sam is away until Thursday; the card waits.

Wednesday. Iris, new to Project A, asks for Sam's trail and walks his Monday in order, collecting the mount on the way. At the rig, the frame-rate project card surfaces, spoken at the printer, routed to the member doing the work it describes; she sets the frame rate. She reads Sam's calibration note, sees nothing that says anyone has touched the rig, and taps it confirmed. She captures all afternoon, four live views, no warnings, and files a completion report. Four green lights: delivered, read, confirmed with her name on the checkmark, an afternoon of work completed on top of them. Dae-ho's card sits anchored to this same rig, ten feet away, invisible to her by its author's addressing choice. Her picture of the rig is stale: the world changed after Sam wrote, the record that would have corrected her was scoped away from her, and nothing in the room says her picture has drifted. Her data is silently off.

Thursday. Sam returns and asks the room the arrival question; triage hands him three cards, and the two that spent Wednesday ten feet apart now sit side by side. He drops the watch on its shelf slot and Priya's request closes without another word: the loop closed across two days, no conversation needed. At the rig, the camera Dae-ho named sits a degree shy of Monday's calibration; he re-runs the board, finds every take skewed, and Iris re-captures on the fresh calibration. The repair lands two days after the fault. Sam freezes the lesson into structure: "Before any capture, re-run the board," confirmed as a Depends_On rule that surfaces before every capture note. He archives Dae-ho's card as handled.

Friday. Priya leaves for the weekend talking in one breath: the watch drains fast when logging; the soldering station's USB-C is fraying. The room splits the breath into two cards, an info note at the shelf slot and a warning at the station; at the door she looks back at where they landed and signs the placement with a yes. After lunch the warning reaches Dae-ho ahead of everything at the station, ahead even of the standing tips note; he swaps the cable and his sentence supersedes the warning within four hours, against the two days his own directed card waited. Renata prints one more plate; the printer shows one card, hers, still true, and the nozzle question never enters her mind.

5 · Implementation

The system section is an instantiation, not a load-bearing engineering claim: it shows that the loop the Concept section argues for can actually run on hardware people can wear today. Its technical core mirrors AsyncReality's Causality Graph. Where Fender and Holz's graph makes replayed events causally coherent for a later viewer, the Memory Graph keeps accumulated utterances current and answerable for a later arriver; and where their graph is written by event capture, ours is written by a structuring pipeline that turns one breath of speech into typed, linked, placed, signed cards. The graph governs surfacing; the pipeline is its writer; the LLM appears only inside the pipeline, as a proposer whose every output a human confirms.

5.1 Platform and anchors

The prototype runs on Meta Quest 3 in passthrough, built in Unity with the Meta XR SDK, as a functional proxy for lightweight AR glasses: the target form factor has position tracking and audio I/O but no outward-facing task cameras, and AsyncReality established the same proxy precedent. Shared spatial anchors persist across sessions and users, so a card placed on Monday is world-locked in the same spot on Wednesday for a different wearer. Places are represented as named zones, volumes bound to anchors and labeled once at setup (printer, capture bench, device shelf, soldering station); a note anchors to the zone the speaker stands in, and the words name the thing. The system is SLAM-only by design: no object recognition, no scene understanding. The camera is a locator, not an observer. Objects enter the record by being named in the utterance (the P2S, the watch, camera three of the rig), which keeps the sensing burden minimal and the privacy posture defensible.

5.2 Capture

Capture is the headset microphone plus cloud speech-to-text. The interaction is hold-to-speak: press, say the note while continuing to work, release. The design target is the one-breath discipline from the scenario: Priya's single Friday utterance about the shelf and the soldering iron is the intended unit of input, one uninterrupted spoken span, produced without leaving the task. Everything downstream exists so that this one breath is enough; the speaker never types, files, or addresses anything by hand unless they choose to override a proposal.

5.3 The structuring pipeline

The pipeline is where the LLM lives, and it lives there strictly as a proposer. It performs three proposal steps over the transcript. Segmentation: one breath may contain more than one note; Priya's utterance becomes two proposed cards, a shelf restock info card and a soldering-station warning card, each with its own anchor. Metadata proposal: for each segment the LLM proposes an anchor zone (from the speaker's position and named referents), an audience (broadcast, project, or directed), and an urgency level (info or warning). Link inference: correction language ("actually," "swapped back," "ignore the earlier note") proposes a Supersedes edge; prerequisite language ("before you print," "read this first") proposes Depends_On; reference language ("about Sam's note," "re the watch") proposes Replies_To.

None of these proposals takes effect on its own. Every card and every link is shown back to the speaker for confirmation, and nothing enters the shared record unsigned: the confirmed card carries its author's name as a signature, which is what later makes a named archive and a Confirmed_by acknowledgment meaningful acts by identifiable people, and what keeps the who of the arriver's questions answerable at all. Anchor confirmation uses a look-back placement confirm: the proposed card renders in place at its proposed anchor, the speaker glances at it, and a single tap accepts, or a drag relocates it. The Friday beat depends on exactly this step; Priya's look-back is the moment the warning becomes anchored where Dae-ho will walk four hours later.

5.4 Graph store and surfacing

The store is a directed typed graph, synchronized across users through a cloud backend and summarized below without formalism.

NodesNote cards (signed, transcribed, anchored); zones; people (as authors, addressees, confirmers); projects. Two memory types only, object and project, defined by what a note is anchored to and what it outlives; person and time are queries over the graph, not node types.
Structural edgesAnchored_to (note to zone), left_by (note to person), directed_to (note to person, the routing case), part_of (note to project).
Semantic edgesSupersedes (correction, version currency), Depends_On (prerequisite ordering, read-first), Replies_To (threading), Confirmed_by (acknowledgment, makes reception visible; reverse awareness, for the leaver).
AttributesAudience (who may see) and urgency (when and how loudly), orthogonal axes. Urgency has exactly two levels, info and warning; there is no blocking level anywhere in the system.
SalienceSurfacing order combines recency, urgency, dependency ordering (a Depends_On target surfaces before its dependent), and version resolution (a superseded note is hidden behind its successor).
LifecycleA note leaves default surfacing by supersession, by named archive (the creator, the directed recipient, or a resolver who leaves their name), or by persistence expiry. There is no bare delete; the record is append-mostly and every removal has an author.

Surfacing is query execution. The five traversals are the complete retrieval vocabulary:

notes_atAll visible, active notes anchored to the current zone, salience-ordered. The default encounter mode; runs continuously as the wearer moves, feeding the passive dot layer of §5.5, never auto-expanded cards.
trailOne person's notes in temporal order with their anchors; walked physically. Iris's Wednesday onboarding.
threadThe closure of a note over Replies_To and Supersedes edges; a conversation reconstructed across places and days. Priya's watch negotiation.
recentEverything visible since the wearer's last visit; the arrival summary.
for_meActive notes with a directed_to edge to the wearer; the directed core of triage. How Sam's Thursday pool assembles.

Arrival triage composes traversals rather than resting on for_me alone: the arrival scene's "what do I need to know" returns directed items, the arriver's own project's routed notes, and any warnings, ordered by salience; for_me by itself is only the directed core.

5.5 Interaction techniques as implementation choices

Several presentation decisions are implementation choices of this prototype, explicitly not part of the concept, and other instantiations could choose differently. Display has exactly two states: from afar, an anchor shows a dot; walk up, and it expands into a readable text card at its thing. When several cards share one anchor they fan out, each holding its own visual position. Nothing pops up by inferred intent, because the system never infers what you are about to use; which card gets read is settled by where the reader stands and which thing they work with, exactly as with paper sticky notes. Both configurations share this passive layer, and in both, every card carries its own words and its raw timestamp. On top of it, the full configuration does exactly three active things. It folds: superseded notes sit behind their successor in a version history the reader can unfold, which is how Iris can see both Renata's swap and Sam's original if she wants the story. It carries: project notes travel to where their project's work continues. It speaks: arrival triage answers with warnings first and directed items, and author labels make the person query answerable. Density tuning beyond this, such as collapsing a very crowded anchor into a count badge, is future refinement explicitly outside the study build. These choices tune presentation density; the claims of the paper do not rest on them.

5.6 Study apparatus

Two apparatus modes support the evaluation. For Study 1, capture is wizarded: audio and location are genuinely recorded while the leaver works, but no live pipeline runs behind the interaction; the recordings are retained as the test corpus for the deferred segmentation benchmark (the Plan's §4 TODO), which can run whenever reviewers or the system section need the number. For Study 2, the system runs for real over a pre-populated graph: the frozen seed set, curated once from Study 1 utterances plus built text seeds (display is text; voice lives only at capture), about 34 notes including superseded chains, drifted declarations, and notes addressed to other people. The set loads into one note database served under two configurations, full and stripped (anchored-only), so the conditions differ in structure alone: the ablation is a config flag, not a second room. The seed loader and the ablation switch (graph reasoning off, raw anchored cards only) are part of the system build. So is the logging apparatus: every card expansion is a logged event (which card, when, where the participant stood), and head pose gives a read-dwell proxy, recorded with consent as study measurement the system never consumes; the deployed privacy stance of no gaze or dwell inference is about what the system acts on, not what the study records.

6Evaluation

The claim has a leaver side and an arriver side that can never be in the room at the same time, so the evaluation is cut in two and chained. Study 1 (RQ1) tests whether awareness records get made at all when externalizing costs one breath; its real utterances, transcribed and curated once, become the frozen seed set, a room installed identically for every arriver. Study 2 (RQ2, RQ3) tests whether the Memory Graph keeps that arriver's picture of the room current and correct beyond what pinning provides, and maps where the picture fails. No live relay ever runs between the two; one frozen room serves both conditions. The structuring pipeline is deliberately bypassed on both sides (Study 1 wizarded, Study 2 frozen); its benchmark is specced and deferred, below.

Methods lineage

Three anchors. Constraint ablation (Gergle, Kraut & Fussell 2013): remove one capability from a full system, pre-declare which indicators should move and which should stand still before any data are collected, and read the mechanism from where compensation appears; their main experiments also ran at a single point well above threshold rather than walking a parametric ladder, the precedent for our one-setting-per-cell rule. Process over outcome (Monk & Watts): people compensate, so outcome alone is insensitive; the compensation behaviors themselves are predictions, one per item family. Hidden-profile construction as instrumentation (Convertino et al.): planted situations with scriptable correct actions, severity pilot-calibrated toward the 36–46 percent success band so no item floors or ceilings, and misreadings located behaviorally in the action log rather than by self-report. Ambiguity is always situational, a world that changed after the leaver departed, never artificially vague wording. A solo arriver is the honest configuration: a live partner would smuggle same-time interaction back into the setting under test.

Study 1: the leaver (RQ1)

Eight to ten participants, within subject, two visits at least a day apart, counterbalanced: one visit speaking in place (wizarded capture), one with phone and the lab Slack; both visits wear the headset, so device burden is constant and only the channel differs. Each visit is about forty minutes of real lab work under mild time pressure. Cover story: morning shift, an unknown afternoon successor; recording is never mentioned; externalizing stays optional and slightly costly, as in life. Nine planted situations per visit (isomorphic A/B sets), typed caused / given / discovered, each mapped in advance onto the Study 2 item it feeds (the plain facts, the correction pair at the core of a pile-up, the stale-able declarations, the routed dependency, the warning), plus the addressing probe and the carve control that should stay on Slack in both arms.

Measures, four. Externalization: said fully, partly, or not, with an exit walkthrough ("show me what you noticed") separating did-not-notice from noticed-and-stayed-silent; only the latter counts. Completeness: every said record coded for the four elements an arriver needs, its thing, its place, its time anchor, and who it is for; the registered prediction is that channel messages, composed later at the door, lose place and time, and this coding doubles as seed quality control, since a record missing its referent cannot become a card. Placement: spoken at the thing in the moment versus batched into one composed message at the end of the visit, a discrete video-coded signature. Cost: time away from the task, word counts, NASA-TLX. Handover: recordings transcribed and curated once into text cards on the note schema, one build sheet for the ~34-card room; classes the participants under-produce are written as text and logged as built; the raw audio is retained as the deferred benchmark's corpus.

The pipeline benchmark (deferred)

Not load-bearing for a concept paper, and bypassed by design in both studies; specced and deferred. When the number is needed: run the writer offline over the retained Study 1 corpus against two coders' gold labels, reporting segmentation boundary accuracy, object-versus-project filing, anchor proposal, audience and urgency proposal, link inference (supersession, replies), capture-to-card latency, and, because nothing enters the shared record unsigned, the correction rate per card, the edits a human must make before signing. Anchoring reliability on Quest 3 (does a card resurface in the right spot across sessions and relocalizations) moves into piloting.

Study 2: the arriver (RQ2, RQ3)

Sixteen new participants, one visit each, cast as a newcomer taking over the Rig project mid-week; two groups of eight, between subjects, because a planted situation teaches its lesson on first contact and a second room cannot un-teach it. Both groups walk into the same frozen room, about 34 text cards with every note-schema field filled; the ablation is a configuration flag over one note database. The passive layer is shared: cards are world-anchored, a dot from afar, an expanded text card on approach, fanned out where several share an anchor, and nothing is ever surfaced by guessed intent. The full version adds exactly three active behaviors, it folds superseded cards behind the current one, carries project notes to where their project's work continues, and speaks (arrival triage, warnings first, directed items), plus author labels and the person query; the stripped version is the passive layer alone. Pinning already works at the thing; everything beyond pinning is what is on trial, and the registered tie on the plain-reading items is what shows the differences come from the graph.

Difficulty is set by five countable dials: records piled on one spot, unrecorded changes after the last record, benches between saying and needing, projects sharing a zone, and authors interleaved; time splits into pile-up and drift on purpose, because many records and a moved-on world are different problems. Crossing the dials with the graph's mechanisms yields seven crossings that genuinely strain something, and the ten scored items plus the arrival scene instantiate exactly those seven, each at one realistic difficulty setting. Families and their pre-registered predictions: plain reading (tie; the control and the fairness check), pile-up (supersession; wrong walks and card-shuffling expected in the stripped group), world-moved-on (drift; the full version converts blind trust into verify-then-act, and the stripped group errs confidently, because nothing in a pinned note says it aged), wrong bench (routing and audience; the stripped group never meets the note, and path data shows it), buried warning (triage; time-to-warning stretches when reading order follows space), who-did-this (authorship; unattributed text gives "who" nothing to stand on). The failure map that answers RQ3 is read from the error types and the compensation signatures per crossing.

Measures in three layers. Outcomes: each item's action against a correct-action script written before piloting, on the four-step scale (skipped it; never found what it referred to; found it but read it wrong; got it), two blind coders, families pooled, no single-cell significance chasing, error types kept. Process: consulted-before-acting, navigation path, wasted work (re-checking settled things, extra walking, questions to the experimenter), arrival coverage and precision, time-to-warning; consultation evidence layered as expansion logs, head-dwell proxy, the wizard's live sheet, and video, with head pose recorded by the study as measurement while the system never consumes it. Debrief: cued, item by item over the seven families, four questions each (what did you conclude, how did you decide, which cards did you use, how sure are you), feeding the understood-for-the-right-reason coding and the registered calibration read of confidence against correctness. Plus NASA-TLX and a late, empty "anything for me?" trust probe. If pilot effects run small, the pre-set rule is to grow to twelve per group before concluding. Deliberately not run, and said so in the paper: parametric ladders, an author ladder beyond the who-did-this contrast, a third arm, deployment, and Replies_To or Confirmed_by as claims (Confirmed_by usage is logged, exploratory).

7 · Discussion (+ Related Work deltas)

The drift limit: what authored feedthrough cannot know

Authored feedthrough carries exactly what the author knew and said at act time; the world keeps changing after the note is signed. The Memory Graph maintains currency against authored change: when a later note contradicts an earlier one, supersession folds the record into its current state. What it cannot do is maintain currency against unauthored change. A note that was true when spoken and false when read is the drift cell of the evaluation (E8's nudged stand, the two DRIFT items), and it is the week's Wednesday, stated exactly: Iris confirms the calibration note and still captures with the data feed silently off; the repair arrives Thursday, two days late, as a pooled triage rather than a next turn. Read under awareness, Wednesday is not a failure of mutual belief; it is stale state, an awareness failure. Iris's four green lights (delivered, read, confirmed, completion report) show the note was delivered, read, and confirmed; none of them can show that the world still matches it. The evaluation reports the drift honestly: RQ3's failure-mode read names which cells break and how (stale, superseded, routed, warning-order), and the world-moved-on items test whether a visible timestamp and the trail of later activity convert blind trust into verify-then-act. Whether drift is a residual failure rate to engineer down or the in-principle boundary of any authored-record medium is the verdict the studies deliver; either answer is a contribution.

The unwitting author

The drift limit has an intake face. Capture is deliberate utterance: nothing enters the shared record unsigned, and nothing is recorded that was not spoken to the system. Authored feedthrough therefore restores only what passes through intentional externalization, and consequential communication, the awareness a working body gives off for free, has no authored substitute: the author must first know they acted. A person who changes the world without knowing it, who nudges a fixture or borrows a cable and thinks nothing of it, writes nothing, and the room stays silent about the change. The nudged rig is exactly the severed consequential channel of the Framing tab's figure, and no note can reopen it. This is a stated boundary of the concept, not a flaw of the system: Spatial Memory covers what people know they know and are willing to say; declining ambient change detection is choosing the authored-feedthrough channel deliberately, with the privacy stance attached, because ambient detection belongs to a different, surveillance-adjacent design space we decline.

Room versus whiteboard

Dillenbourg and Traum's persistent whiteboard, one task, one surface, one hour, is prior evidence that a shared artifact can carry coordination across time. But a persistent surface is a log, and awareness is "knowledge that must be maintained and kept up to date" (G&G, p. 416). A multi-project room across weeks changes the economics. Persistence alone yields accumulation without relevance; every visitor would face everyone's residue. The pile-up, crowding, and voices dials of the Plan tab's matrix name exactly what breaks at that scale. This is why the medium needs the graph: Triage's purpose-relative salience, Audience scoping, and Supersedes pruning are what make persistence survive scale; maintenance is what turns a surface into a state. The whiteboard result predicts fact notes travel well and inference notes travel poorly, which is exactly the fact-as-control, inference-as-trap prediction Study 2 tests, pre-declared under RQ2 and read for failure modes under RQ3.

What the room accumulates

By Friday the room holds two layers. On top is maintained state: what is in what condition now, one current card per fact, kept current by supersession. Behind it is history: how each thing came to be so, the how, when, and who of the arriver's questions, traversable through folded versions, timestamps, and trails, and never in the way of the current picture. The third and most interesting deposit belongs to neither layer. Thursday's lesson, frozen as a Depends_On read-first rule on the rig, is a future-directed element, the kind G&G exclude because belief about the future takes inference rather than perception (p. 421), hardened into structure: the team's hard-won procedure becomes a structural property of the place that binds arrivers who never met the people who learned it. Convertino et al.'s common-ground measures survive here as instrumentation lineage for the evaluation, not as a frame the concept needs.

The addressing default

The week argues for a default: address the place, not the person. The setting's signature reader is unknown-next (dimension D2): a place-addressed note is addressed to whoever the workspace makes it relevant to, which is what E8's place-versus-person probe measures in the wild and what E9's carve-out protects. Dae-ho's only directed note misaddresses an encounter-class fact as an episode-class message and waits two days; Priya's broadcast warning at the soldering station reaches him in four hours. Directed notes remain correct for genuinely episode-class content; the design's position is that the system suggests the addressing, via Routing and audience proposal, and the human decides at signing.

Privacy stance, recapped

No passive capture, no ambient recording, no bare delete: every removal carries a name. Every card is an authored, signed utterance; the camera serves as locator, not recorder; trail visibility defaults to direct collaborators with broader access opt-in. The lifecycle rule that archiving requires a name (creator, directed recipient, or resolver) keeps accountability symmetric with authorship. One scoping decision belongs here. G&G Table II's presence and location history column asks "Who was here, and when?" and "Where has a person been?", and it is the privacy-sensitive column of the framework. Spatial Memory answers it only at authored granularity: the person query and the trail traverse signed acts, never sensed movement, so "who was here" means "who signed something here," and continuous presence tracking stays out of scope for exactly the reasons this stance names.
