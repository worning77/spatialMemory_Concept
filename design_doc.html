Spatial Memory: Design Document

Structured as the paper skeleton: each section states what the paper section must accomplish, then the design content that feeds it · v3 · 2026-06-30 · CHI 2027

One sentence: Spatial Memory lets a shared physical workspace carry what its people chose to say to whoever needs it next, where it happened: deliberate voice notes captured at the effort of a single spoken sentence, anchored in place, structured in a Memory Graph, and surfaced at the moment of encounter.

Abstract draft. three beats: problem, system, evidence

Shared physical workspaces are asynchronous by default. Collaborators share machines and benches but rarely share the clock, and the judgments that matter, such as a swapped nozzle, a stale calibration, or a fragile joint, die unspoken. They die because sending a message costs the person in flow more effort than the observation seems to justify. We present Spatial Memory, a paradigm in which deliberate voice notes are captured at the effort of a single spoken sentence, anchored where they were spoken, and organized in a Memory Graph that keeps each place showing its current state rather than a pile of history. We evaluate the concept in two chained studies. Leavers (N≈10) work hands-busy tasks, and their real utterances seed the rooms that arrivers (N=12–16) later walk into. In-place voice capture raised in-flow externalization over phone-and-channel practice at lower effort, and graph structure beat raw anchoring on two things a flat anchored list cannot do: act on a place's current state, and deliver a warning that had to travel from where it was left to where it was needed.

1Introduction lead with the proposition and genre; then motivate with a concrete instance, ground the problem as handoff failure (deferred to §2.1), kill camera and channel, name the reframe, earn the graph (Moves 1-7)

Thesis and genre. This paper proposes a theory and builds a system to show it holds. The theory is that grounding, in Clark and Brennan a synchronous, two-party achievement, can be relocated into a space-anchored, accumulated structure through three substitutions: choosing a recipient becomes anchoring to a place; describing the referent becomes spatial co-location, so deixis is restored across time and the reader stands where it was said; and accepting with a live reply becomes a later person's action on the world, a deferred and world-mediated acceptance. Spatial Memory is the system that instantiates these substitutions, and the evaluation confirms each one and reports the boundary where it breaks, namely that asynchrony removes repair and cannot complete mutual belief. The system is simple by design; the theory is the load-bearing contribution. This is a concept proposal confirmed by evaluation, not an open study of a system's role, and the motivation that follows earns the proposition from the failure it repairs. The synchronous version of this relocation is From Speech-to-Spatial (Kim et al., ISMAR 2026), a live shared view; ours is the asynchronous case. In one line, they are a spatial Zoom and we are a spatial Slack (§2.5).

Setting. Shared physical workspaces are asynchronous by default. In labs, machine shops, clinics, and kitchens, collaborators share equipment and benches but rarely share the clock: the morning person leaves before the afternoon person arrives. The room is the only collaborator present across every shift, and it remembers nothing.

The problem is a handoff with no handoff. In the gap between shifts, the judgments one worker forms and the next worker needs are lost. This is a well documented and costly failure wherever work is passed across time, and we ground it in that literature in §2.1. What makes the physical workspace worse is that it has no handoff ritual at all: no shift-change meeting, no named successor, and the thing to pass on is only a small judgment formed in the middle of a task. A worker swaps a nozzle, notices a fragile joint, or leaves a calibration stale. Each observation is true, each matters to whoever comes next, and each dies unspoken.

Why the observation dies: effort, not forgetting. The worker does not forget. They run a rational calculation. To send the observation they would have to choose a recipient they often cannot name, describe a physical thing whose critical property is invisible, and switch out of a task their hands are in the middle of. That effort exceeds what the single observation is worth to them, so it is never sent. Everyone makes the locally correct choice, and the information is lost anyway. In Clark and Brennan's terms these are the costs of grounding, registering an addressee, formulating without a gesture to point with, and leaving a reader to reconstruct the context, and a shared-space-but-never-shared-moment medium makes all three spike (§2.5).

Why existing tools cannot carry it

Two responses look obvious, and both fail. A camera cannot help, because the content is not an observable event but an authored judgment: a swapped nozzle, a stale but valid-looking calibration, and a fragile joint look identical on video. The judgment exists only in a person's head and must be spoken. This is the inversion of AsyncReality, whose asymmetry is that the receiver cannot perceive what changed; ours is that the author will not spend the effort to send. A messaging channel cannot help either, because this information fails it on at least two of three gates.

G1 · Recipient
Is the next reader genuinely unknown at compose time?

Channels demand a named addressee → broadcast spams, DM guesses, or nothing gets sent.

G2 · Referent
Does it point at a physical thing with an invisible property?

Lossy to describe in words, lossy to re-ground when read. "The 0.4 nozzle... on which printer?"

G3 · Moment
Is it useful only when standing at the object?

Phone delivery lands placeless and mistimed: before it matters, and away from where it matters.

The strongest opponent is the per-fixture channel. Real labs have #3dprinter and #mocap channels, and a channel does approximate the recipient gate: it is the machine's mailbox. What it cannot fix:

FailureWhy the channel loses
Delivery (G3)By the time the next person stands at the machine, the message is days deep in scrollback. Nobody re-reads a channel standing at the rig before pressing record.
Log vs. stateA channel is a time-ordered log, but the room needs the current state at a place. "Is the recalibration still pending, or did someone already do it?" is scrollback archaeology on Slack. In the Memory Graph it is one supersedes edge. The next person sees the current answer, not the history.
CompositionStill requires phone-out, mid-pack-up, hands full of tripods.

We do not compete with Slack on its home turf. Known-recipient, bounded, episode-class messages stay on Slack (§3), and the scenario shows this carve explicitly.

Reframe. Keep the irreducible part, which is that the human authors the content. Remove the part that can be removed, which is the addressing effort: naming a recipient, describing the referent, and switching out of the task. This is now buildable, because persistent shared anchoring, head-worn capture, and on-device language models that transcribe and segment speech have converged, and the instrumentation is first-person, worn by the collaborator rather than fixed to the room. The worker speaks one sentence in place. The worn device captures what they chose to say. The room carries it forward, anchored where it was spoken. We call this paradigm Spatial Memory, and the structure that organizes it the Memory Graph.

The proposition, now earned. The reframe is precisely the thesis stated at the outset: the addressing effort the worker declines to pay is the cost of grounding, and removing it relocates grounding into space. Choosing a recipient becomes anchoring to a place; describing the referent becomes spatial co-location, where the reader stands where it was said and deixis is restored across time; and accepting with a live reply becomes a later person acting on the note, a deferred and world-mediated acceptance. The figure below traces one such relocation across a shift gap, and the three substitutions organize the concept in §3 and the evaluation in §8.

Anchoring is not enough. Once the room remembers everything, the next arriver drowns, because anchoring alone reproduces the spam problem, now in space. The record has to be structured, not merely pinned. The problem decomposes into four sub-problems, tagged A–D throughout this document. A, B, and C are failures of the mute room; D is the failure Spatial Memory itself creates and must then solve, and that two-level shape is what justifies the Memory Graph rather than plain anchoring.

TagSub-problemVictimSystem answer
A · CaptureThe person holding a judgment is mid-task. Addressing and sending a message costs more effort than the message is worth to them, so it is never sent ("leaver-in-flow")the next person who hits the hidden statelow-effort anchored voice + auto-segmentation
B · Cross-project collisionOne project's invisible change to a shared fixture harms a different project's user. The two share nothing but the objecta stranger at the same fixtureobject memory that outlives projects; warnings crossing project lines

Contributions. This paper contributes (1) the theory that grounding, a synchronous two-party achievement in Clark and Brennan, can be relocated into a space-anchored, accumulated structure through three substitutions, the paper's load-bearing contribution; (2) Spatial Memory, the paradigm that instantiates that theory for place-based asynchronous collaboration, together with the Memory Graph, the minimal structure that turns anchored notes into current state and routes them along the relationships of place, person, and project; (3) a working system on a head-worn device as a proxy for future lightweight AR glasses, and a week-long scenario that dramatizes how the substitutions play out across shifts; and (4) an evaluation that confirms the theory's three substitutions and reports the boundary where grounding lands only one-way, rather than testing the prototype's usability.

2Related Work outline only. five subsections, each closing on "we build on / extend," never "they fail to"

Outline for the paper's §2. Each subsection states the point it must make, its key references, and how Spatial Memory positions against it.

2.1 Co-located asynchronous collaboration and the cost of handoff

Point: shift-based and co-located-async work loses the information one worker holds and the next needs, and that loss is documented and costly. This is the home of the problem (C1) and the handoff grounding promised in §1.
Key refs: handoff/handover failure (Patterson et al. 2004; clinical omission rates), hidden-profile under-sharing (Stasser & Titus 1985), articulation work (Schmidt & Bannon 1992), awareness in shared workspaces (Gutwin & Greenberg 2002; Dourish & Bellotti 1992), co-located-async coordination through artifacts (Bardram 2009).
We build on: we take the documented handoff failure as our problem and extend it to a setting with no handoff ritual at all.

2.2 Spatial and world-anchored annotation in AR/MR

Point: anchoring information to places is established; structure over accumulated anchored information is not. Anchoring is the easy half.
Key refs: world-stabilized annotations (Gauglitz et al. 2014), place-based messaging and digital graffiti (McGookin et al. 2014; E-graffiti), async MR and the causality graph (Fender & Holz 2022).
We build on / extend: we build on anchoring as a primitive and add the missing half, a structure that resolves state and routes information across places. Distinguish explicitly from AsyncReality: their asymmetry is receiver-cannot-perceive, so room cameras suffice; ours is author-will-not-spend-the-effort, so the person must be the witness.

2.3 Low-effort and in-flow capture

Point: capturing while the hands are busy is hard, and the cost of capture conflicts with the primary task; voice is the lowest-effort channel for an in-flow note.
Key refs: voice vs ink vs keyboard micronote capture; capture-in-flow and its conflict with manual work (Rigaud et al. 2022); cost of grounding (Clark & Brennan 1991).
We build on: we build on in-flow voice capture and remove the addressing effort, not the authoring.

2.4 LLM structuring of natural language

Point: language models can segment and type free speech into structured records, the means that turns one spoken sentence into anchored, audience-tagged, supersedable cards.
Key refs: existing §2.4 LLM references already in reference.bib (verify and extend).
We build on: we build on LLM segmentation as a component, scoped to a small, stateful graph rather than open-ended generation.

2.5 Grounding, deixis, and spatial reference

Point: the theory we build on is Clark and Brennan's grounding: collaboration runs on common ground, built by joint work whose cost shifts with the medium. Recent AR work grounds spoken reference in space, but only synchronously.
Key refs: grounding and its costs (Clark & Brennan 1991), deixis (Bolt 1980), and From Speech-to-Spatial (Kim et al., ISMAR 2026), which grounds spoken referents on a live shared view via an object-centric relational graph and persistent AR guidance.
We build on / extend: From Speech-to-Spatial grounds a reference on a live shared view in real time; we relocate grounding across time, into a shared physical space that different people visit at different moments, where acceptance is deferred to a later person's action. In one line, they are a spatial Zoom; we are a spatial Slack.

3Concept paper's job: define Spatial Memory as the three grounding substitutions made concrete — capture, anchor, structure, surface — and the information class it serves

Three substitutions: the through-line

Spatial Memory is the theory's three substitutions made concrete, and the rest of this section hangs off them. Each substitution converts a cost that a synchronous conversation pays live into a property of a space-anchored, accumulated record.

Grounding moveSubstitutionWhere it lives in the concept
choose a recipientanchoring to a place: the note attaches to the place, not a named addresseethe anchor step of the loop; the Anchored property; object memory's place anchor
describe the referentspatial co-location: the reader stands where it was said, so deixis is restored across timethe surface step; encounter-class information; the invisible referent that words alone would lose
accept with a live replydeferred acceptance: a later person acts on the note, a world-mediated acceptancethe structure and surface steps under accumulation; supersession as the trace of that later action

The loop below is how the system runs these substitutions; the four properties, the two memory types, and the low-effort stance that follow each realize one or more of them. The boundary the theory carries, that deferred acceptance is not the mutual belief a live reply secures, is what §8 confirms and bounds.

The idea: one loop

Spatial Memory is a loop with four steps. A worker captures a judgment by speaking one sentence in place, at low addressing effort. The system anchors it to the place where it was spoken. The Memory Graph structures it, assigning type, audience, persistence, and supersession. When the next person approaches, the place surfaces its current, relevant state. In grounding terms, anchor performs the recipient substitution, surface performs the co-location substitution, and a later person's action on what surfaces performs the acceptance substitution. No single step is new: notes have been anchored in space before, and messages have been typed and filtered before. The contribution is closing the loop for encounter-class information in shared physical work, so that a judgment spoken in flow reaches whoever needs it next, where it happened.

The four properties

PropertyMeaning
AnchoredNotes attach to the place where they were spoken, such as a machine, a bench spot, or a shelf
AccumulatedThe space builds up knowledge over time, from multiple people and projects
LayeredNotes carry persistence levels (ephemeral / project-lived / permanent) and audiences
NavigableThe record is structured: versioned, filterable, and traversable, not a pile of pins

Encounter-class vs. episode-class

Encounter-class information has low individual value, high cumulative value, a spatial referent, and an unknown next reader. Channel tools price it out, so it dies. Episode-class messages have a known recipient and bounded content, so Slack is the right tool and Spatial Memory never sees them. This carve is the paper's claim boundary: we displace nothing that works, and we catch what today has no carrier at all.

Two memory types, and why only two

A memory type is defined by two questions: what is the note anchored to, and what does it outlive.

TypeAnchored toOutlivesExample
Object memorya place: a fixture or a movable's home spotany project (institutional)"0.6 nozzle is in" on the printer; "recalibrated for the A volume" on the rig
Project memorya Project spanning many placesnothing; lives and dies with the project"shoot the A capture at 120fps" travels from the printer to the mocap rig

Person is not a third type. Nothing is anchored to a person, and "person memory" outlives nothing in particular. Person, like time, is a query over the graph, not a storage type: whose trail, addressed to whom, since when. This definition is also the pre-emptive answer to "why is there no person/time/task memory?"

Low effort, not no effort

The low-effort stance is the author's side of the recipient substitution: anchoring to a place removes the addressing effort, but the human still authors every memory. Judgment is irreducible, and every memory is a deliberate utterance: no ambient inference, no gaze or dwell logging, no telemetry (validated by a 60-scenario scoring exercise in which every inferred-event design was rejected). What the system removes is only the addressing effort: recipient selection, referent description, and app switching. The cost of forming and speaking the sentence remains. This is also the privacy stance: not surveillance, but capture of chosen speech.

4Memory Graph paper's job: the structuring component between capture and surfacing; the build spec for the room's memory

The Memory Graph is the room's memory: the structuring component that sits between capture and surfacing. Structurally it is a small, typed, supersedable store, an advanced filter over the notes a room accumulates. Its value is not the data structure, which a plain table could implement, but its position in the loop: it turns a stream of in-place utterances into the current, routed, audience-scoped state that surfaces at the moment of encounter.

The whole graph in one sentence: a note is pinned at a place, signed by a person, optionally belongs to a project, and can supersede an older note. Everything below is the build spec for that store.

Definition
Nodes (4)Note · Place (a SLAM zone: a fixture, or a movable's home spot; "objects" are just named places) · Person · Project
Edges (5)anchored_to (note→place) · left_by (note→person) · directed_to (note→person/role) · for_project (note→project) · supersedes (note→note)
Note properties (2)audience (who should hear it) · persistence (ephemeral / project-lived / permanent)
Queries (4)notes_at(place) · for_me(person) · trail(person) · project_state(project)

The five operations

OperationWhat happens
Fileone utterance → object card and/or project card, audience and persistence inferred, and the speaker classifies nothing
Surfaceapproaching a place replays the current state of its notes (after supersession), filtered to the viewer's audience, and including notes routed here by an active project even if they were left elsewhere
Supersedea newer note replaces an older one, so the place shows state, not log
Filteraudience + relevance decide what an arriver actually hears, in order
Expireephemera die on schedule; project notes die with the project

SLAM-only constraint. The camera is a locator, never a recorder: no visual content, no small-object recognition. Movables get a home-spot Place, are named in the utterance ("the tracker on the end"), and ride project memory across stations. This constraint is precisely why both memory types are necessary: the zone anchor covers the encounter, and project memory covers everywhere else the warning must travel. Only the pair covers both.

The hard part is building it from speech. The data model is trivial; turning a messy in-flow utterance into it is not. The system structures each utterance against the current graph: it infers the note's type, its audience from project membership, and whether it supersedes an existing note. That stateful structuring, not the data model, is the engineering, and we isolate this component's contribution in the evaluation (§8).

6Scenario paper's job: one week, the three substitutions dramatized across shifts as near-misses with victims — never a feature tour

Cast: Sam (Project A motion-capture; seeds Monday's notes, returns Thursday), Iris (joins Project A midweek to run the capture while Sam is away), Renata (Project B; the printer), Dae-ho (Project C; the parts bin), Priya (Project D; the tracker shelf).

Setting

A shared maker lab: one Bambu 3D printer, a motion-capture rig in the corner, a shelf of shared test devices, a soldering station, a pegboard tool wall, and five workstations. Several projects share the printer, the rig, and the shelf, but the people rarely overlap in time, so the room is the one constant that sees every shift. Five collaborators pass through across the week, each on a different project: Sam runs a Project A motion-capture; Iris joins Project A midweek to run the capture while Sam is away and has never touched his setup; Renata (Project B), Dae-ho (Project C), and Priya (Project D) each pass through the shared fixtures on unrelated work. No one is addressing anyone in particular, which is exactly the condition under which a placeless channel makes situated knowledge cost more to send than it is worth.

Monday: Sam seeds three invisible notes

Sam is setting up a Project A motion-capture later in the week. It needs a stiff custom marker-mount, so at the Bambu he pulls the 0.4 nozzle, threads in abrasive carbon-fiber filament, and drops in a hardened 0.6. He starts the print and says into the room without breaking stride: "0.6 hardened nozzle and CF in for the A mount. Swap back to 0.4 and PLA before you print anything fine. This one runs till about three, don't cancel it." The sentence pins itself to the printer. He crosses to the mocap rig, re-runs the calibration for his capture volume, and, because from across the room the rig looks exactly as it did yesterday, says: "Recalibrated the rig for the A volume this morning." Pinned to the rig. On his way out he takes a shared tracker off the shelf and starts it logging a battery test; its screen is dark and it looks idle, so he says: "This tracker is mid-log, screen is off but it is running, leave it." Pinned to the shelf.

Before he leaves Sam adds one more sentence, but files it to the project rather than to a place: "For whoever runs the A capture, shoot at 120fps, not the default 60, or the fast motion blurs." It is not pinned to a fixture; it is a project memory that travels with Project A, while the first three are object memories bound to the printer, the rig, and the shelf. Four sentences, three naming a place and the fourth a project, and none naming a recipient, because Sam has no idea who prints, captures, or reaches for a tracker next. This is the recipient substitution: choosing an addressee is replaced by anchoring to the place, so each note costs Sam a breath rather than the price of a message that, on a channel, would have gone unsaid.

Tuesday: three strangers, three near-misses

Renata, on Project B, comes to print a fine calibration plate. As she lines up the job, Sam's note surfaces at the machine that the 0.6 and CF are still loaded; on the default profile a fine plate through an abrasive nozzle would have been two hours of ruined print. She swaps the 0.4 and PLA back, prints, and says "0.4 and PLA back in, profile matches again." Her sentence replaces Sam's on the printer, so the machine shows what is true now rather than a stack of history. This is the co-location substitution doing the reader's half of the work: Renata reads the note where and when it is actionable, at the machine at the moment of use, so the referent she would otherwise reconstruct from words is simply present in front of her.

The same afternoon, Priya reaches for a tracker on the shelf and Sam's note surfaces that the one on the end is mid-log. A logging tracker and a sleeping one are identical, so a camera watching the shelf all day could never tell them apart; the difference lived only in Sam's head until he spoke it, and she takes a different one. Dae-ho goes for his parts bin, finds it gone from its spot, and a note at the empty spot points him to the shelf where Sam had set it aside to clear the print bench. Each near-miss is avoided at the place the mistake would have been made.

Why not just a channel? The lab does have a printer channel, and Sam could have posted there, but by the time Renata is standing at the machine that message is days deep in scrollback and no one re-reads a channel before pressing print. A channel is a log, not a state: it cannot answer whether the 0.4 is back in right now. On the printer that is a state, superseded the moment the nozzle is swapped, so the next person always sees the current answer.

Wednesday: Iris joins, and grounding slips

Iris is new to Project A this week, brought in to run the capture while Sam is away, and she has never touched his setup. She asks the room for Sam's trail, and it walks her through his week in order: the printer where the mount was made, the rig he recalibrated, the shelf and its logging tracker. Ten minutes replace what would otherwise have been a scramble of messages or would have gone unasked. Person is not a stored type here but a query over the accumulated record, and the trail is that query rendered as a path through the room. One of the notes waiting at the rig was never spoken at the rig: Sam said it at the printer, but because it belongs to Project A and Iris is running the A capture, the project memory followed the work to her here, and she sets the frame rate to 120fps. Carrying a note to where the work continues rather than to where the words were said is the one thing a place alone cannot do.

Then grounding slips. At the rig Iris reads Sam's note, "Recalibrated the rig for the A volume this morning," and, being on Project A, takes it to mean the rig is calibrated and ready for her. But "the A volume" was Sam's own capture volume and marker set, not a blessing for anyone on the project, and Iris, new, has no way to know that. She loads her markers and records; the rig throws no warning and the calibration reads valid, so her data comes out subtly, silently off, the kind caught only days later in processing. The note reached her, which is the delivery half working, but "the A volume" meant one specific setup to Sam and "ready for me" to Iris, and across the gap there was no one to catch the difference in the moment. This is exactly the boundary the concept names: deferred acceptance stands in for a live reply, but it is not the mutual belief a live reply secures, and because the medium is asynchronous there is no repair turn to catch the mismatch as it happens. Delivery worked; grounding did not.

Thursday: Sam returns, and the repair comes late

Sam returns and walks his stations, and each place shows him its current state rather than a history to scroll. At the printer, Renata has already swapped the nozzle back, so her card stands current and his own is collapsed behind it. At the rig he resets the volume and his Monday notes fold away, superseded, the places clean again. Seeing that Iris's recording is off, he finds her at the rig, they trace it to the calibration, and he leaves a sharper note this time: "Rig is set to my A volume, marker set B. If that is not your setup, recalibrate before you record." Iris re-runs her calibration and re-captures, and it comes out clean. The acceptance the concept promises does arrive, since a later person acts correctly on the corrected note, but it arrives two days late and at the cost of a wasted session. Relocating grounding into space recovers most of what a live partner would have carried, and it does not recover the immediacy of repair.

Friday: different people, different mail

By Friday the room holds dozens of notes from five people across five fixtures, and if it surfaced all of them, anyone walking in would drown, the same overload problem now in space. Pinning notes in place is the easy half; letting a room accumulate a week and still hand each person only their slice is the half that needs structure, and that structure is the Memory Graph. Each person asks the room the same question, "anything for me?", and each gets a different short answer, with superseded notes collapsed, expired ones gone, and everything not for them filtered away. Iris gets Sam's clarification and nothing else. Renata gets a single note that her plate is ready. Sam gets two, Iris's question about the re-capture and Renata's note that his configuration had been in the way. Dae-ho's bin, Priya's tracker, and the other projects' churn surface for no one they do not belong to.

What the week shows

The week runs the three substitutions in order and shows the boundary of the third. Monday is the recipient substitution: four judgments externalized at the cost of a breath because each anchors to a place rather than an addressee. Tuesday is the co-location substitution: three strangers read the right note at the moment and place of use, with supersession keeping the record a current state rather than a log. Wednesday and Thursday are deferred acceptance and its limit: a later person's action stands in for a live reply, which recovers grounding for Renata cleanly but only one-way for Iris, whose misreading no repair turn could catch until Sam returned. Friday is what makes the accumulated record usable at all, the Memory Graph triaging a full room into per-person slices. Each person occupies the same space and yet meets a different room.

8Evaluation paper's job: test the concept's claims (not the prototype's usability) through converging evidence; every number traces to real in-flow speech. DRAFT: study tasks are placeholders and may change

Evaluation rationale

Spatial Memory contributes a concept, not a system optimization, and we evaluate it accordingly. Following arguments that paradigm-level contributions are mis-served by usability benchmarks \cite{olsen2007evaluating, greenberg2008usability, wobbrock2016research}, we do not ask whether our prototype is fast or learnable; we ask whether the claims the concept makes hold. Because the value we claim, that a shared space can carry encounter-class information and surface it as current state at the point of use, has no established yardstick, our evaluation must do three things that a technique evaluation receives for free from its task: it operationalizes that value into measurable outcomes, it attributes any benefit to the mechanism rather than to confounds, and where possible it shows the alternative to be not merely worse but structurally limited.

We distribute this across complementary evidence. The filmed scenario carries integration and longitudinal scope, a full week of accumulated use that no single controlled study can hold, and each study in turn isolates one claim. A technical evaluation establishes that the pipeline structures real in-flow speech. Study 1 tests whether removing the addressing effort of capture changes behavior. Study 2 holds the notes constant and varies only the Memory Graph, to measure what the structuring component is worth under a week of accumulation. The scenario integrates; the studies isolate.

The study in grounding terms

The evaluation reads the study through Clark and Brennan's grounding, exactly as the theory frames it. First it places the medium: on the eight properties of a medium (copresence, cotemporality, reviewability, and the rest), a shared physical space is a new profile, asynchronous but with copresence of place, which the Framing tab lays out and which predicts our costs. The proposition is then three things; the study confirms each and reports where it breaks, with two kinds of evidence: numbers on the system pieces, read as grounding costs, and interviews on whether grounding was reached.

ThingGrounding cost it addressesTest and evidence
1 · the author's cost drops (leaver)start-up and formulation cost: finding an addressee and putting the note into words (the recipient substitution: addressing becomes anchoring)Study 1 — in-place voice versus phone-and-channel, both wearing the headset; externalization rate, time-off-task, NASA-TLX (behavioral leg)
2 · the reader's cost is kept low (arriver, the graph)understanding cost: least collaborative effort forbids simply dumping the author's saved cost on the reader (the co-location substitution, plus routing under accumulation)Study 2 — SM-full versus anchored-only over a week's accumulation; state-resolution and cracked-bracket traps, time-to-relevant, overload
3 · grounding is reached, or not (deferred acceptance)acceptance: async has no live reply, so a later person's action on the world stands in for it (the load-bearing, Slack-only substitution)Study 2 interviews on the same arrivers, coded grounded versus broke (qualitative leg that bounds the theory)

Two grounding measures sit over the three Things. Total collaborative cost (author plus reader) should drop and redistribute versus the baseline, least collaborative effort made visible. And grounding reached, the grounded-versus-broke rate, is the outcome: an arriver is grounded when they acted correctly for the intended reason, and grounding broke when they misread, were right for the wrong reason, were unsure, or matched a mismatched criterion, coded by two coders in a focused thematic analysis. The honest boundary: asynchrony removes repair and cannot complete mutual belief, so we report where grounding is only one-way. That boundary is what makes this a grounding paper rather than a system test, and it supplies the discussion. In short, the technical leg confirms the mechanism is realizable and the qualitative leg confirms and bounds the theory.

Goal. Study 1 tests whether people externalize a judgment once the addressing effort is removed (claim C2). Study 2 tests whether the structuring component, not anchoring alone, makes the accumulated record usable (claim C3). The two are chained: the voice notes real participants produce in Study 1 are structured by the real pipeline and become the rooms that Study 2 participants walk into, and the same corpus serves as the technical-evaluation benchmark.

Cut by design: deployment. No one wears today's headsets through real workdays. The premise that lightweight all-day glasses arrive is the paper's opening bet on the industry trajectory, not the study's burden. The studies evaluate the marginal-effort claim: given that you are already wearing the device, is speaking in place better than the channel?

The chain

Study 1 utterances (real, recorded and located) feed real LLM segmentation, run offline (RQ3 is measured here, on this corpus), which produces a curated frozen seed set: one representative real note per trap, plus experimenter-authored fillers to complete the week (other projects, a cross-team note). Every Study 2 participant then faces the same pre-seeded rooms. We use no live relay: if each arriver consumed a different leaver's notes, no two participants would face the same room and the conditions would not compare. Collect first, curate once, freeze. Reported honestly: seed notes were drawn from Study 1 participants' recordings and supplemented by researcher-authored notes to constitute a full week.

Study 1 (Leaver): capture (tests C2) · N ≈ 10

Cover story: "You are the morning shift; someone you do not know takes over this station this afternoon. Do the job, and handle anything you notice however you normally would." Recording is never mentioned, so it stays optional and costly, exactly like life. Both conditions wear the headset (passthrough), so device burden is constant and the only variable is the capture channel: in-place voice (speak in place; wizarded, with audio and location genuinely recorded and no live pipeline) versus phone and channel (phone in pocket, lab channels available). Within-subject, two matched task variants, counterbalanced.

Task (draft, may change): a hands-busy assembly with mild time pressure, for example collect a draft print, assemble a bracket, solder a connector, and stage a logging phone for a 48-hour test. Several planted encounters per variant, some caused by the participant and some discovered, including at least one hidden defect a later worker must be warned about (a hairline-cracked bracket among the staged parts):

Post-task manipulation-check interview ("walk me through what you noticed") separates did not notice from noticed but did not record, since only the latter is the phenomenon. An exit question guards novelty inflation: "in your own lab, would you still have said these?"

Study 2 (Arriver): structure (tests C3) · N = 12–16 · Quest 3

Cover story: "You are the afternoon shift, new to this project. Get up to speed, then complete a few jobs." Conditions are within-subject on two matched rooms (Week A and Week B, counterbalanced): SM-full (the graph on: supersession, audience filter, project routing, triage) versus anchored-only (the same notes pinned in place but the graph off: a superseded note hangs beside the one that replaced it, nothing is filtered, expired, or routed, and there is no ordering). This is the ablation. It holds capture, anchoring, and surfacing constant and varies only the structuring component, so any difference measures that component's marginal share in the loop. Anchored-only is what world-anchored annotation does today (we cite that work), so it is a real baseline, not a strawman. About 35 to 40 notes per room, including superseded pairs, a project note that routes across places, and notes addressed to others, so structure has real work to do.

#Task (draft, may change)What it tests
T0"Anything you need to know before starting?"triage time, relevant items found (filter benefit, secondary)
T1 · statea job at a place whose earlier warning may be still live or already resolvedstate resolution: does the participant act on the current state, both when the issue is still live and when it has been resolved?

The two headline traps are T1 and T2, because each isolates something the filtering alone does not cover: resolving a place to its current state, and surfacing a warning on the right part at the moment of use. T1 tests both error directions, acting on a stale issue and redoing a resolved one. T2 is the cleanest contrast, since anchored-only leaves the crack warning buried among the raw notes while SM-full surfaces it on the very bracket being picked up. The ablation measures how much the structuring component is worth once capture, anchoring, and surfacing are already in place.

The grounding layer (RQ4, deferred acceptance). On the same arrivers, an interview asks whether they reconstructed the author's meaning, whether standing at the place helped, where they were unsure, and whether they did what the author intended. Two coders code each encounter grounded (acted correctly for the intended reason) versus broke (misread, right for the wrong reason, unsure, or mismatched criteria) in a focused thematic analysis. This is where the deferred-acceptance substitution is confirmed and bounded: a later person's action stands in for the live reply async cannot provide, and because async removes repair, we report honestly where grounding lands only one-way rather than completing mutual belief.

9Discussion paper's job: name the portable concepts, be honest about limits — the ammunition list

ThreadThe argument to make
Log vs. stateGeneralize the channel critique: communication tools store time-ordered logs, but physical work needs current state at a place; supersession is the primitive that converts one into the other. Portable beyond AR.
Camera as locatorThe privacy stance as a design position: today's 6DoF needs cameras, but the system uses them only for pose — no content, no recognition, no inference. When camera-less positioning matures, the paradigm transfers unchanged.
Encounter-class informationThe portable distinction: a class of workplace information priced out by every channel tool. Future systems (proactive agents, ambient capture) can be positioned by which class they serve.
GeneralityOne paragraph claiming the class of place: shift-based clinics, commercial kitchens, theater shops, makerspaces, field stations — anywhere work is physical, shared, and time-sliced.
