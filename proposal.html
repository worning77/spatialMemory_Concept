Proposal

The paper in five blocks. Each block gives, in plain language and in logical order, what the section will say, then the questions still open. Written 2026-09-22 for the glasses version. The concept flow is on the Concept tab and the study derivation is on the shared sheet.

In one line. A room used in shifts loses the small facts that matter only at the thing, because leaving word costs the leaver everything and pays them nothing. Spatial Memory lets the room keep three kinds of memory, what was said at a thing, what a project needs next, and who was where. Five solutions keep them true as the room fills up. It runs on glasses that open the camera only when the wearer stops. A week-long scenario shows it runs, and a study against Slack shows that arrivers get a correct picture.

1Intro

Many people share one physical workspace without sharing time. Maker labs, research labs and studios run in shifts. What gets lost there is not the big decisions, which have meetings and documents, but the small facts that matter only when a later person reaches the thing. The nozzle was swapped. The scale was re-zeroed. The camera rig was nudged.

Face to face you learn what others did in three ways. You watch them, you hear them, and you read the state of the things they touched. Once the other person has left, the first two are gone and the third shows the present, not the past. A nudged rig looks calibrated.

The person who knows does not leave word because leaving word is expensive in every way Clark and Brennan's cost ledger names. You have to stop what you are doing and pick up a phone. You have to decide who needs it, and often nobody in particular does. You have to put into words where and what, when a gesture at the thing would have said it. The channel is not the place, so the reader has to rebuild the place from words. The payoff goes to someone else, later, while the cost is yours now. Under that ledger silence is the rational choice, and the small facts keep dying.

We propose Spatial Memory, a room that keeps its own past in three kinds of memory. What was said at a thing stays at the thing, one sentence with no recipient and no description of where, because the place supplies both. What a project needs next follows the project's work to its next place. Who was where is recorded by the wearer's own glasses and released by the wearer, the one kind nobody has to author. Together they answer the six past-tense questions of workspace awareness. As the room fills up, five things make those answers hard to keep true, and we give one solution for each. It runs live on glasses that open the camera only when the wearer stops.

What we did, and the contributions. We shot a week in a maker lab on the running system, ran a study with sixteen to twenty-four arrivers against the same notes delivered over Slack on tasks that push each difficulty to its limit, and benchmarked the speech-to-card step. If it runs, a small leaver study seeds the room and measures what speaking in place costs. Four contributions follow. The concept, three kinds of memory and five solutions, which carry workspace awareness across time through the place itself. A working system on glasses, with a released body trace as the memory nobody authors. The scenario. The study against Slack with the benchmark.

Positioning sentence, two candidates. Against a system. AsyncReality replays what the room did while you were away, and Spatial Memory lets the people who were there say what the next person needs to know, and remembers who they were. Against a class. AR annotation asks where to put a note, and Spatial Memory asks what a room should do with a week of them.

Questions. Which positioning sentence, after the positioning table is drawn. Whether the intro lists the six questions or leaves them to the concept. Whether the camera property belongs in the intro or only in the system section. The final numbers for the "what we did" paragraph. Whether "presence memory" reads as a kind of memory to a cold reader.

2Concept

The section opens where the intro left off, with the state of things as the only survivor and the past it cannot show. Gutwin and Greenberg list six past-tense questions, quoted as they stand.

ElementQuestion
Action historyHow did that operation happen?
Artifact historyHow did this artifact come to be in this state?
Event historyWhen did that event happen?
Presence historyWho was here, and when?
Location historyWhere has a person been?
Action historyWhat has a person been doing?

Two are about the thing, three are about a person, and one is about an act that someone may or may not have mentioned.

The base. Standing at the thing, you say one sentence. The note is pinned there and the next person who walks up sees it. Four steps. Capture, one breath, anywhere, so that a note said while walking lands on the thing it names, else where it was said, else at the nearest station with a flag. Anchor, the place. Structure, the five solutions below. Surface, a dot from afar, the card on approach, several cards fanning out at one anchor.

Three kinds of memory, told apart by what they are anchored to and how long they live. Object memory is anchored to a place or object and outlives any project ("0.6 nozzle is in"). Project memory is anchored to a project, follows its work across places, and dies with it ("set the cameras to 40"). Presence memory is anchored to a person at the places they stopped and passed. Nobody authors it. The room records it from the wearer's own sensors, the wearer decides at the end of the session whether to release it, and it is kept for a window rather than forever. The first two are active, someone says them. The third is passive, the room records it. Together the three are Spatial Memory, the six questions answered in the room.

Five things make awareness hard in a shared space, and we propose one solution for each. The memories answer the questions on a quiet day. As the room fills with records and people, five things get in the way.

The first four work on notes. The fifth cannot, because no note exists, so it works on presence memory. Record, with inertial odometry between stops and a short camera look when the wearer stops. Release, the wearer's decision at the end of the session. Segment, so that stops become presence at anchors and walks become transits past things. Surface, in the same room. The trace knows who passed, not what happened. Speaking is consent by itself, and the trace needs an explicit release.

None of these five relations is new. Versions, threads, audiences, signatures and read-first are the metadata of any messaging tool, and Slack has them all. Spatial Memory lets them take effect at the place. Folding happens on the printer, carrying goes from one bench to another, dimming and signatures apply where you stand, and one relation is added that no channel can hold, who was here. Collecting along a relation (a project's notes, a person's trail, the recent) and picking out what is for you on arrival follow from the relations and are described with the system.

Questions. "Presence memory" as a name and as a third kind, or two kinds with the trace as a record type outside them. Whether the trace is introduced at the fifth difficulty, as here, or with the three kinds. Whether read-first stays under Route or gets a line of its own. Length, since the material is five pages and AsyncReality's concept is two.

3Scenario

A week in the maker lab, shot on the running system. Monday, Sam seeds the room with a note at the printer, the rig and the shelf, and one said while walking about the blocked aisle. Tuesday, Renata swaps the nozzle back and her note folds over Sam's, a box moves to the shelf, and the watch logs on the shelf. Wednesday, Iris reads a rig note that is literally true and no longer right, because Dae-ho carried boxes past the tripod and said nothing, and the capture fails. Thursday, the room shows at the rig who passed after the last note, the cure-time card said at the glue bench waits at the sanding bench marked read first, and Iris checks before acting. Friday, the week's project notes go with the project. Each beat exercises one solution and a closing table says which. The plot stays as written in August. Thursday changes, one beat is added, and the illustrations become captures.

4System

The system makes the three memories exist on glasses and the five solutions take effect at the place. The section follows the order things happen, from what we assume about the room to where the code runs.

Assumptions. A workspace with fixed stations (benches, machines, shelves) that do not move between visits. One scan of the room, done once, gives the map and the station anchors. Moving objects are out of scope. Every person wears their own glasses. The glasses have a camera that can be switched on and off and no positioning of their own, so all positioning below is ours.

Records get in two ways. Active. The wearer says a wake word and one sentence, and the glasses record audio, position and time. Live transcription and an LLM turn the breath into cards, propose the anchor (the thing the sentence names, else where it was said, else the nearest station with a flag) and fill the fields, object or project, who it is for, warning or not, who, when. The LLM chooses only among the anchors the scan produced. The card appears at the place within seconds and the speaker confirms or fixes it there. Passive. Between stops the wearer's position comes from inertial odometry on the glasses. At a stop the camera turns on for one or two seconds, a few frames are matched against the scan, position and heading are corrected, and the camera turns off. The odometry also guards the camera, since a match that lands far from where the odometry says the wearer is gets rejected. At the end of the session the path is smoothed with every stop as a fixed point, stops become presence at stations, walks become transits past things, and the wearer decides whether to release it.

Records are kept as a plain graph, with records, stations and people as nodes and the relations between them as edges. This is a design decision matched to the five solutions, not a contribution, and any messaging tool keeps the same thing under the hood.

Two things need no solution of their own. What is here, shown as a dot from afar and cards on approach, fanning out at a shared anchor, placed at the anchor plus a fixed offset, facing the viewer, never over a panel or a work surface. What is for me, on arrival.

Where it runs. The glasses carry the microphone, the IMU, the camera bursts and the display. A phone in the pocket runs the odometry, the matching, the transcription, the LLM calls and the graph. Nothing runs after the fact except the benchmark. A table lists, for every feature, whether it is live on the glasses, live on the phone, or wizarded in the study.

Numbers the section reports. One benchmark table for the speech-to-card step, with segmentation, anchor proposal, object versus project, audience and warning, links and per-card corrections, against a rule-based and a manual ground truth. One paragraph on the trace in our room, with station attribution, dwell error, anchor hits for notes said while walking, the share of time the camera was on, and the one contrast that answers why not just photograph each stop. Odometry and matching are cited components and are not ablated or compared to prior systems.

Questions. RayNeo details to confirm on the device, which are IMU access and rate, frame access when toggled, world-anchored text, and phone link bandwidth. Matching against the scan without markers, or AprilTags at the stations, or both. Ground truth for the trace paragraph, a Quest worn alongside or a lab mocap. Whether the on-the-spot confirmation is a tap, a word, or nothing.

5Study

The scenario shows the system runs. The study asks what the scenario cannot answer. When a real person arrives in a room that carries its past, do they get a correct picture of what happened, and where does the picture fail. That is a question about awareness, not effort. PaperToPlace, the nearest study in shape, promises less effort and measures usability, workload, time and head movement, never whether the cook did the right thing. Our promise is the picture, so we measure first the action against a scripted truth, second how they got there (checked before acting, which cards opened, which route walked), third whether they accepted the room's judgments (the top card is current, dimmed cards are not theirs, a signature is worth believing, someone passed here after the last note), and last usability, workload and preference.

Against what. The same notes delivered the way people do it today, as messages in a Slack channel on a phone. Both groups wear the glasses, the Slack group sees no cards, and both enter the same prepared room and do the same tasks. Between subjects, eight per group, growing to twelve if the effects are small.

Which tasks. One per difficulty, each pushed to the point where its solution is needed and built so that without the solution the information cannot be reached. A pile of records on one thing. A note said at one bench and needed at another. Several projects on one bench with one dimmed card that concerns you. Five people's notes in one zone with a conflict between two of them. The nudged camera, where the last note is literally true and nobody said anything. Plus a control task with one card on one thing, and an arrival scene, five minutes in a full room. Resume and trail are the same room under two prompts, split across participants. The tasks come from the shared sheet, dimension by dimension, and the crossings table says what must not be staged together.

How a session runs. An hour. Arrival first. Six task blocks in counterbalanced order, each observed for the action, the time, the check before acting, and a short debrief on what they concluded and why. The trust questions sit inside the blocks. At the end everyone leaves word for the next person and decides whether to release their own trace. Findings are reported per task, with proportions correct and the test that fits, time and workload with repeated-measures statistics and effect sizes, and the debriefs coded.

The leaver side, optional. The claim that speaking in place costs less than Slack and yields records the pipeline can use rests on the design and the benchmark. If time allows, a small seeding study makes it a measured claim. Four to six people work in the lab for thirty to forty minutes in each condition, speaking in place or using a phone and Slack. Capture is real, confirmation is simulated, and no display is needed. It reports notes per hour, words per note, time off task, four-element completeness and workload, and its recordings become the seed room and the benchmark corpus. If it is skipped, the team seeds the room through the live pipeline and the leaver side is a stated limitation.

Questions. Whether YY and Darren accept the Slack group after the August agreement on a single group. Whether the IRB needs a modification. Whether one stale top card goes into the pile task. Between subjects as planned, or two matched wings of the lab for a within-subjects design if the pilots show small effects. Whether the leaver study runs, and before or after the arriver pilots.
