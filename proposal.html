Proposal

The paper in five blocks. Each block gives, in plain language and in logical order, what the section will say, then the questions still open. Written 2026-09-22 for the glasses version; updated 2026-10-05 with the four model papers, the reader study (under Studies, to discuss with YY), and the device decision of 2026-09-29 (Quest 3 for the study, Aura for the scenario, no camera image reaches the system). The concept flow is on the Concept tab and the study derivation is on the shared sheet.

In one line. People who share a workspace in shifts lose the small facts that matter only at the thing, because leaving word costs the leaver everything and pays them nothing. Spatial Memory lets the room keep three kinds of memory, what was said at a thing, what a project needs next, and who was where, and keeps them true as the room fills with five solutions, one for each way a shared room defeats a newcomer. It runs live on a commodity headset and reads only the headset's own position and the microphone; no camera image reaches the system. A week in a maker lab, shot on the system, shows it runs; a study of arrivers against the same notes without the solutions shows whether they get a correct picture and where it fails.
Models. The paper's body follows Sensible Agent (UIST 2025): a framework stated as named modules with a dataflow figure, design implications grounded in data, a working prototype on a commodity headset, and a study whose claim rests on correctness and effort rather than speed. Three more papers each set one part. The concept, the studies and the scenario stay as decided; the models only say how they are written up.

1Intro

Many people share one physical workspace without sharing time. Maker labs, research labs and studios run in shifts. What gets lost there is not the big decisions, which have meetings and documents, but the small facts that matter only when a later person reaches the thing. The nozzle was swapped. The scale was re-zeroed. The camera rig was nudged.

Face to face you learn what others did in three ways. You watch them, you hear them, and you read the state of the things they touched. Once the other person has left, the first two are gone and the third shows the present, not the past. A nudged rig looks calibrated.

The person who knows does not leave word because leaving word is expensive in every way Clark and Brennan's cost ledger names. You have to stop what you are doing and pick up a phone. You have to decide who needs it, and often nobody in particular does. You have to put into words where and what, when a gesture at the thing would have said it. The channel is not the place, so the reader has to rebuild the place from words. The payoff goes to someone else, later, while the cost is yours now. Under that ledger silence is the rational choice, and the small facts keep dying.

We propose Spatial Memory, a room that keeps its own past in three kinds of memory. What was said at a thing stays at the thing, one sentence with no recipient and no description of where, because the place supplies both. What a project needs next follows the project's work to its next place. Who was where is recorded by the wearer's own glasses and released by the wearer, the one kind nobody has to author. Together they answer the six past-tense questions of workspace awareness. As the room fills up, five things make those answers hard to keep true, and we give one solution for each. It runs live on a commodity headset that stands in for lightweight AR glasses, and it reads only the headset's position and the microphone; no camera image reaches the system.

What we did, and the contributions. We shot a week in a maker lab on the running system, ran a study with sixteen arrivers (eight per condition, growing to twelve) against the same notes at the same anchors without the five solutions, on tasks that push each difficulty to its limit, and benchmarked the speech-to-card step. If it runs, a small leaver study seeds the room and measures what speaking in place costs. Three contributions follow. The concept, which carries workspace awareness across time through the place itself, derived from the six questions to three kinds of memory to five difficulties and their solutions, each grounded in prior work. The system on a commodity headset, with a released trace as the memory nobody authors, shown running in the scenario. The study against the same notes without the solutions, with the benchmark. If the reader study runs (see Studies), the derivation is checked against what readers expect and the pipeline is conditioned on their examples, which is the ground Sensible Agent gives its framework.

Positioning sentence, two candidates. Against a system. AsyncReality replays what the room did while you were away, and Spatial Memory lets the people who were there say what the next person needs to know, and remembers who they were. Against a class. AR annotation asks where to put a note, and Spatial Memory asks what a room should do with a week of them.

Questions. Which positioning sentence, after the positioning table is drawn. Whether the intro lists the six questions or leaves them to the concept. Whether the no-image property belongs in the intro or only in the system section. The final numbers for the "what we did" paragraph. Whether "presence memory" reads as a kind of memory to a cold reader.

2Concept

The section is one derivation in four steps. What the later person needs to know. Who can supply it, and how, which gives three kinds of memory. What breaks them as the room fills, which gives five difficulties. What restores each, which gives five solutions. Every step cites the work it rests on, so the concept is a synthesis, not a coinage.

Step 1, what the later person needs to know. Gutwin and Greenberg list six past-tense questions of workspace awareness, quoted as they stand. In a room used in shifts the actor is gone, so watching and hearing are cut and only the state of things remains, which shows the present. The six questions are what the present cannot show.

ElementQuestionAbout
Artifact historyHow did this artifact come to be in this state?the thing
Event historyWhen did that event happen?the thing
Action historyHow did that operation happen?an act, said or unsaid
Presence historyWho was here, and when?a person
Location historyWhere has a person been?a person
Action historyWhat has a person been doing?a person

Step 2, who can supply it, and how. Only the person who was there can answer, and there are two ways an answer can reach the room. They can say it, or the room can record what their body gave off. What is said can be anchored to two things, the thing it concerns or the work it belongs to. Crossing the two ways with the two anchors gives three kinds of memory and one empty cell.

The base of all three is delivery where the fact applies. A note said at the thing needs no recipient and no description of where, because the place supplies both, and a note said while walking lands on the thing it names, else where it was said, else at the nearest station with a flag. Together the three are Spatial Memory, the six questions answered in the room. Prior work supplies each piece and none of the whole. Notes anchored in place are AR annotation and situated notes (AnchorNote, Luo's placement studies). Records that follow a project are threads. Body traces released for others are the ubicomp presence line and PEARL's in-situ movement analysis.

Step 3, what breaks them as the room fills. On a quiet day the three memories answer the six questions. Each difficulty is a count that rises with use, and each threatens one question.

Step 4, what restores each. One solution per difficulty, each a relation kept on the record and a way of showing it at the place. The relations are the metadata of any messaging tool. Slack has versions, threads, audiences and signatures. What is new is that they take effect at the place, and that the fifth is a relation no channel can hold.

The first four work on what was said. The fifth works on what was recorded, in four steps of its own. Record, from the headset's own tracking, and on glasses from inertial odometry with sparse fixes (InrtlAR, SparseVIO). Release, the wearer's decision at the end of the session. Segment, so that stops become presence at stations and walks become transits past things. Surface, in the same room. The trace knows who passed, not what happened. Speaking is consent by itself, and the trace needs an explicit release. Collecting along a relation (a project's notes, a person's trail, the recent) and picking out what is for you on arrival follow from the relations and are described with the system.

Questions. Whether the empty cell in step 2 (recorded, anchored to the work) is worth a sentence or a cut. "Presence memory" as a name. Whether the prior-work column in step 3 is enough for the concept to count as a synthesis, and which citations each row needs. Whether read-first stays under Route or gets a line of its own. Length, since AsyncReality's concept is two pages.

3Scenario

A week in the maker lab, shot on the running system. Monday, Sam seeds the room with a note at the printer, the rig and the shelf, and one said while walking about the blocked aisle. Tuesday, Renata swaps the nozzle back and her note folds over Sam's, a box moves to the shelf, and the watch logs on the shelf. Wednesday, Iris reads a rig note that is literally true and no longer right, because Dae-ho carried boxes past the tripod and said nothing, and the capture fails. Thursday, the room shows at the rig who passed after the last note, the cure-time card said at the glue bench waits at the sanding bench marked read first, and Iris checks before acting. Friday, the week's project notes go with the project. Each beat exercises one solution and a closing table says which. The plot stays as written in August. Thursday changes, one beat is added, and the illustrations become captures.

4System

The section is organized by the concept's claims, not by the pipeline. It walks the same list as the concept, the room, the three kinds of memory, the five solutions, and for each says what we decided, why, and what it costs. How the pieces are wired, on which device, with which components, is one short subsection at the end. A reader who has just finished the concept should recognize every heading.

The room

Decision. Stations are fixed things (benches, machines, shelves), the room is scanned once, and the scan gives both the map for positioning and the anchors for records. Moving objects are out of scope. Why. A shift-shared workspace is a place where the fixtures stay and the people change, so a one-time scan holds for weeks, and anchoring to fixtures is what lets a record be found by anyone. Cost. Nothing can be anchored to a mug, and the scan must be redone when a bench moves.

Said memory

Decision. One wake word and one sentence, anywhere. The sentence becomes a card that lands on the thing it names, else where it was said, else at the nearest station with a flag. An LLM proposes the segmentation and the fields, choosing anchors only from the scan, and the speaker confirms or fixes the card on the spot. Object memory and project memory differ only in the anchor the LLM proposes. Why. The cost ledger: no recipient to choose, no place to describe, no channel to open, and the confirmation keeps authorship with the speaker. Cost. A note said far from what it concerns can land wrong, which the flag makes visible.

Presence memory

Decision. The wearer's position comes from the headset's own tracking, which the app reads as a pose stream and nothing else. At the end of the session the stream is segmented, stops become presence at stations and walks become transits through zones, and the wearer decides whether to release it. The app never receives a camera image. Why. The system is the study's instrument, so tracking has to be the most stable thing available, and a commodity headset gives that today. The glasses this is written for will give the same pose from inertial odometry with sparse camera fixes (InrtlAR, SparseVIO), so the headset stands in for them and the trace does not depend on how the pose is made. Reading only the pose keeps the privacy story whole: the room learns where people stopped because they released it, and it sees nothing. Cost. On today's headset the pose is made by cameras inside the device that the app does not see, so the paper says "no image reaches the system", not "camera-free". The trace knows who passed, not what happened. It is empty for anyone who does not release it, and it says zones, not objects.

The five solutions at the place

Decision. Records, stations and people are kept as a plain graph with the five relations as edges, which any messaging tool keeps under the hood; the design decision is what each relation makes the place show.

Two display rules serve all five. Cards sit at their anchor plus a fixed offset, face the viewer, never cover a panel or a work surface, show as a dot from afar and open on approach, and fan out when several share an anchor; placement is a rule, not an optimization, because a card is one sentence read once. On arrival the audience and read-first relations are collected at the door into what is for me. Why. Each solution is the smallest thing that keeps one of the six questions answerable as its difficulty rises, and showing it at the place is what makes it different from the same relation in a channel. Cost. Every relation is proposed by the LLM or the trace and can be wrong, which is why the speaker confirms and the arriver can unfold, undim and check.

Implementation

The study runs on a Meta Quest 3 in passthrough, the headset the approved protocol names and whose anchors persist across days; the scenario is shot on XREAL Aura glasses once they ship, to show the form the system is written for. The headset carries the microphone, the tracking and the display. A backend holds the graph, runs the transcription and the LLM proposer, logs the pose stream and the card events, and writes the day's arrival brief into the lab's channel at the end of each day, which is the one place the room meets the tools people use today. The headset client is either a WebXR page or a Unity app: a one-week test of persistent anchors on both, with the room changed between days, decides, and if the page passes it is also the Aura build without porting and the release is a URL. Transcription and the proposer are off-the-shelf models; everything is cited, none is evaluated as a contribution. Everything runs live; only the benchmark is run on recordings afterwards. The section reports one benchmark table for the speech-to-card step (segmentation, anchor proposal, object versus project, audience, links, per-card corrections, against hand labels; and, if the reader study runs, agreement of the relation judgments with readers) and one paragraph on the instrument: anchor re-placement after the room has been changed, and station attribution of the trace in our room.

A camera image at the moment of speech, to ground "this one" and to give the arriver a photo of the state then, is a deliberate omission here: the study does not use it, WebXR cannot capture it, and it belongs to the follow-up on evidence from frames. The paper states the camera policy for the target glasses in three sentences: tracking never depends on the camera; a frame, if the glasses have a camera, is taken only when the wearer speaks to the room, with the light on; the system runs without it.

Questions. WebXR or Unity, from the anchor test. Whether the on-the-spot confirmation is a tap, a word, or nothing. Persistent anchors on Aura for the scenario shoot, or a fresh placement per take. Whether the channel digest appears in the scenario once or stays in the system section.

5Studies

Two roles, two studies. The leaver study asks whether people put the past into the room when doing so costs one breath. The arriver study asks whether a person who arrives later gets a correct picture out of it, and where the picture fails. The scenario has already shown that the system runs, so neither study is about usability. The task derivation lives on the shared sheet, Design x Study Dimensions.

The reader study (online, before the lab studies; to discuss with YY)

Sensible Agent's framework stands on 960 judgments from 40 people about what a proactive agent should do and how; its taxonomy and its prompt examples come from them. Our concept stands on a derivation. The reader study gives it the same kind of ground without touching the two lab studies: it asks readers whether they expect what the derivation predicts, and it gives the LLM its examples.

Participants and material. About forty people with shared-workshop experience, online, fifteen to twenty minutes each. Each reads twenty to twenty-four situations drawn from the scenario and the sheet's cells: a note as the transcript would give it, the station, the author, the time, one or two earlier notes on the same thing, and who the reader is, when they were last in the room and which project they are on.

Questions per situation. Should the room show this to you: now at the door, when you reach the station, or not at all. How it relates to the earlier notes: replaces one, waits on you, is for someone else, none. In which form: a dot, an open card, one line in the arrival brief. The order of three notes at one station. One sentence why.

What it yields. A table of what readers want surfaced, in which form, by situation (Sensible Agent's Table 2). The examples that condition the speech-to-card step and the arrival brief (its Table 1). Agreement between readers, with the cells where they disagree kept for the discussion as the places where the room should hold back. One number for the system section: how often the pipeline's relation judgments agree with readers. Five design implications, each ending with the solution that answers it.

Cost and timing. One to two weeks, participant fees, a web form, an online-survey addition to the IRB. It needs the scenario text, not the system, so it runs alongside the build.

With the leaver study. The two together are the data layer: readers' expectations from the online study, real spoken records from the leaver study. The first grounds the concept and conditions the pipeline; the second gives the benchmark its corpus and its numbers.

Questions. Whether to run it, and whether YY's thematic method applies to the free-text reasons. Whether the situations come only from the scenario or also from the sheet's untaken cells. Whether its results are reported in the concept section as design implications or in the studies section. Whether a leaver-side version (what would you say here, and to whom) is worth adding.

Study 1: the leaver (TBD)

Study 1 measures what speaking in place costs compared with the way people leave word today, and produces the records that seed the room for Study 2. It is optional. If it does not run, the claim rests on the design and the pipeline benchmark, the team seeds the room through the live pipeline, and the leaver side is a stated limitation.

Participants and design. Four to six makerspace-experienced people from outside the lab, each in two sessions at least a day apart, within subjects. In one session the participant can speak in place; in the other they have a phone with the lab's Slack. Order is counterbalanced. Each session is thirty to forty minutes of real lab work with planted situations worth a note; recording is never mentioned, so externalizing stays optional and costly, as in life.

Apparatus. Capture is real: a microphone and the position log record every utterance with its place and time. Confirmation of the card is simulated, and no display is worn, so the participant does not need the AR glasses.

Measures. Notes per hour, words per note, time away from the task, whether each record names its thing, its place, its time and who it is for, and NASA-TLX. The recordings become the seed corpus for Study 2 and the corpus for the pipeline benchmark.

Study 2: the arriver

Study 2 tests the paper's central claim: an arriver in a room that carries its past gets a correct picture of what happened. This is a question about awareness, not about effort. The nearest study in shape, PaperToPlace, promises that spatialized instructions cost less effort and measures usability, workload, time and head movement; it never measures whether the cook did the right thing, because that is not its promise. Ours is, so the primary measure is the arriver's action against a scripted truth.

Design. Between subjects, two conditions. In the Spatial Memory condition the room runs with the five solutions. In the control condition the same notes sit at the same anchors without them: every version visible, nothing carried, nothing dimmed, no names, no trace. The control isolates what the paper adds, since delivery at the thing is already established by prior work, and it is the comparison the approved IRB protocol describes. Both groups wear the headset, enter the same prepared room and do the same tasks. Eight participants per condition, growing to twelve if the pilots show small effects.

Participants and setting. Makerspace-experienced people from outside the lab, cast as a newcomer taking over a project mid-week. The lab is dressed as the scenario's maker lab and frozen with about thirty-four notes, seeded through the live pipeline and curated, plus one released trace.

Procedure. One visit of about an hour, eight tasks. The first is always the arrival scene, five minutes to find out what matters today in the full room. The other seven follow in counterbalanced order, one per cell from the sheet: one card on one thing (the control task); a stack of records on one thing; a colleague's scattered session, asked as a person; a project's needs posted across the room, one of them said at another bench; a shared bench crowded by several projects with one dimmed card that concerns them; five people's notes in one zone with a conflict between two of them; and the nudged camera, where the latest note is literally true and nobody said anything. The last two happen at the same station back to back. Each task is built so that without its solution the information cannot be reached, which is what the control group's behaviour reveals. After the tasks the participant decides whether to keep or release the trace their own headset recorded during the visit, a real switch, and then a cued debrief goes through each task: what they concluded, how they decided, which cards they used, how sure they were. About fifty minutes of tasks, ten of debrief.

Measures. Three layers, then the usual questionnaires. First, the outcome: for each task the action taken, scored against the scripted correct action by two blind coders. Second, the process: whether they checked before acting, which cards they opened, which route they walked, and time, all from the headset log and video. Third, acceptance: inside the relevant tasks, whether they trusted the top card as current, treated dimmed cards as not theirs, believed a signature, and heeded the line that someone passed here after the last note. Last, NASA-TLX and preference.

Analysis. Findings are reported per task. Correctness as proportions per condition with the test that fits the counts, time and workload with repeated-measures statistics and effect sizes, and the debriefs coded for reasons and error types. The result is a map: where the room's answer is reliable, where people re-read anyway, and where the picture fails in each condition.

Questions. Whether Study 1 runs, and before or after the Study 2 pilots. Re-alignment with YY and Darren, who agreed in August on a single condition. Whether one stale top card goes into the stack task. Between subjects as planned, or two matched wings of the lab for a within-subjects design if the pilots show small effects. Whether the nudged-camera task needs the failed capture as its trigger, or the presence line alone.
