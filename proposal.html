Proposal

The paper in five blocks, each with what it says, the decisions behind it, and the questions still open. Written 2026-09-21 on the assumption that the system runs live on glasses. The concept flow is on the Concept tab; the study derivation is on the shared sheet.

In one line: a room used in shifts carries its own past on two channels, what people said in place and where their bodies went, kept true by five mechanisms, on glasses that open the camera only when the wearer stops; a week-long scenario shows it runs, and a study against Slack shows arrivers get a correct picture.

1Intro

Says. People share workspaces without sharing time. Of the three ways to learn what others did (watch them, hear them, read the state of things), the first two are cut once the actor leaves, and the third shows the present, not the past. Leaving word costs the leaver everything and pays them nothing, so the small facts die. Spatial Memory lets the room carry its past: a note said at the thing appears at the thing; a released trace says who was here; a Memory Graph keeps both true. Built on glasses without a continuous camera. Shown in a week-long scenario, tested against Slack.

Decided. Contributions are four: the concept (two channels and the graph as awareness maintenance), the system on glasses, the scenario, the study plus a pipeline benchmark. One positioning sentence against AR annotation and AsyncReality. A "what we did" sentence with scale numbers before the contribution list. Writing follows AsyncReality's structure.

Open. The exact pairing for the positioning sentence (AsyncReality, or the AR-annotation class), decided after the positioning table is drawn.

2Concept

Says. When the other person has left, two of the three ways of knowing what they did are gone: you cannot watch them and you cannot hear them. What is left is the state of the things they touched, and a thing's state shows the present, not the past. A nudged camera rig looks calibrated.

What the later person needs is the past half of workspace awareness. Gutwin and Greenberg list six past-tense questions, quoted here as they stand:

ElementQuestion
Action historyHow did that operation happen?
Artifact historyHow did this artifact come to be in this state?
Event historyWhen did that event happen?
Presence historyWho was here, and when?
Location historyWhere has a person been?
Action historyWhat has a person been doing?

Two of them are about the thing (how did it come to be like this, when), three are about a person (who was here, where have they been, what have they been doing), and one is about an act that someone may or may not have mentioned (how did that operation happen).

The base. Standing at the thing, you say one sentence. No recipient and no description of where, because the place supplies both. The note is pinned there and the next person who walks up sees it. Four steps: capture (one breath, anywhere; a note said while walking lands on the thing it names, else where it was said, else at the nearest station with a flag), anchor (the place), structure (the mechanisms below), surface (a dot from afar, the card on approach, several cards fanning out at one anchor).

Three kinds of memory, told apart by what they are anchored to and how long they live. Object memory is anchored to a place or object and outlives any project ("0.6 nozzle is in"). Project memory is anchored to a project, follows its work across places, and dies with it ("set the cameras to 40"). Presence memory is anchored to a person at the places they stopped and passed; nobody authors it, the room records it from the wearer's own sensors, the wearer decides at the end of the session whether to release it, and it is kept for a window rather than forever. The first two are active: someone says them. The third is passive: the room records it. Together the three are Spatial Memory, the six questions answered in the room. Person and time are otherwise not kinds of memory but ways of asking.

Five things make awareness hard in a shared space. We propose one solution for each. The memories above answer the questions on a quiet day; as the room fills up with records and people, five things get in the way.

CauseMechanismWhat the room doesWhat you see
Stacking: records about one thing pile up over time, only the latest is trueSupersedefolds a record's versions to the current onecurrent on top, history behind
Divergence: said in one place, needed in anotherRoutea project's record follows its work to the next place, or its scattered records are pulled together; read-first ordering rides on itthe card waits at the destination, marked with where it came from

Silence: something happened and nobody saidTracekeeps presence memory: where a body stopped and passed, released by its owner; answers who was here after the last recordwho passed here; a route on the floor; a cue to check before acting

The first four work on notes. The fifth cannot, because the point is that no note exists, so it works on presence memory instead: record (inertial odometry between stops, a short camera look when the wearer stops), release (the wearer's decision at the end of the session), segment (stops become presence at anchors, walks become transits past things), surface (the same graph). The trace knows who passed, not what happened. Speaking is consent by itself; the trace needs an explicit release.

None of these five relations is new. Versions, threads, audiences, signatures and read-first are the metadata of any messaging tool; Slack has them all. What Spatial Memory does is let them take effect at the place: folding happens on the printer, carrying goes from one bench to another, dimming and signatures apply where you stand, and one relation is added that no channel can hold, who was here. Two things follow from the relations without a mechanism of their own and are described in the System block: collecting along a relation (a project's notes, a person's trail, the recent), which is how gathering happens, and picking out what is for you on arrival.

Questions. Presence memory as the name and as a third kind, or keep two kinds and treat the trace as a record type outside them. Whether the trace is introduced at the fifth cause (current) or with the three kinds of memory up front. Whether depends_on stays under Route or gets a line of its own. Whether the three queries are enough, or for_me is just collect along audience. Length: the material is five pages, AsyncReality's concept is two.

4System

Says. The system is what makes the three memories exist on glasses and the five solutions take effect at the place. It is described in the order things happen: what we assume about the room, how records get in, how they are kept, what the five solutions do with them, and where the code runs.

Assumptions. A workspace with fixed stations (benches, machines, shelves) that do not move between visits; one scan of the room, done once, gives the map and the station anchors; moving objects are out of scope. Every person wears their own glasses, watch and pocket phone. The glasses have a camera that can be switched on and off and no positioning of their own, so all positioning below is ours.

Getting records in, two ways. Active: the wearer says a wake word and one sentence; the glasses record audio, the current position and the time; live transcription and an LLM turn the breath into one or more cards, propose the anchor (the thing the sentence names, else the position it was said at, else the nearest station with a flag) and the card's fields (object or project, who it is for, warning or not, who said it, when); the card appears at the place within seconds and the speaker can confirm or fix it there. The proposer chooses only among the anchors the scan produced. Passive: between stops the wearer's position comes from inertial odometry on the glasses, watch and phone streams (InrtlAR); when the wearer stops, the camera turns on for one or two seconds, a few frames are relocalized against the scan (hloc, or AprilTags at the stations), the position and heading are corrected, and the camera turns off; at the end of the session the whole path is smoothed with every stop as a fixed point, stops become presence at stations and walks become transits past things, and the wearer decides whether to release it.

Keeping them. Records, stations and people are stored as a plain graph, the relations between them as edges: supersedes, project, depends_on, audience, author, present_at, passed_near. This is a design decision matched to the five solutions, not a contribution; any messaging tool keeps the same thing under the hood.

The five solutions, each as what it stores and what it shows. Supersede: a supersedes edge; at the anchor the current card sits on top and the older ones fold behind it, expandable. Route: a project edge and a depends_on edge; a project card appears at the station where the project's work continues, marked with where it was said, and read-first cards come before the rest; collecting along the project edge lists a project's notes together. Audience: an audience edge; cards not addressed to you are dimmed, never hidden. Authorship: an author edge; every card carries its author, and collecting along it replays one person's notes in order. Trace: present_at and passed_near edges from the released path; at a station the room can say who was here after the last note and show a cue to check before acting; a person's route can be drawn on the floor with stops as beads; the release switch is the wearer's. Two queries need no solution of their own: what is here (a dot from afar, cards on approach, fanning out at a shared anchor; placed at the anchor plus a fixed offset, facing the viewer, never over a panel or a work surface) and what is for me on arrival.

Where it runs. Glasses: microphone, IMU, camera bursts, display. Phone: odometry, relocalization, transcription and the LLM calls, the graph. Nothing runs offline except the benchmark, which is run on the recordings afterwards. A table lists, for every feature, live on the glasses, live on the phone, or wizarded in the study.

Numbers the section reports. One benchmark table for the proposer (segmentation, anchor proposal, object versus project filing, audience and warning, links, per-card correction rate; against a rule-based and a manual ground truth). One paragraph of trace validity in our room (station attribution, dwell error, walk-and-talk anchor hits, the fraction of time the camera was on, counted from stops). Odometry and relocalization are cited components; nothing is ablated or compared to prior systems.

Questions. RayNeo specifics to confirm on the device: IMU access and rate, camera frame access when toggled, display of world-anchored text, phone link bandwidth. Relocalization source: hloc against the scan (no markers) or AprilTags at stations (robust), or both in the paper. Ground truth for the validity paragraph: a Quest worn alongside, or a lab mocap. Whether the speaker's on-the-spot confirmation is a tap, a word, or nothing.

5Study

Says. The scenario shows the system runs. The study asks the question the scenario cannot answer: when a real person arrives in a room that carries its past, do they get a correct picture of what happened, and where does the picture fail. That is a question about awareness, not about effort. PaperToPlace, the nearest study in shape, promises that instructions cost less effort and measures usability, workload, time and head movement; it never measures whether the cook did the right thing, because that is not its promise. Ours is. So the first thing we measure is whether the arriver acts as someone who knew: the action against a scripted truth. The second is how they got there: did they check before acting, which cards did they open, which route did they walk. The third is whether they accepted the room's judgments: that the top card is current, that dimmed cards are not theirs, that a signature is worth believing, that someone passed here after the last note. Usability, workload and preference come last.

Against what. The same notes delivered the way people do it today: as messages in a Slack channel on a phone. Both groups wear the glasses (the Slack group sees no cards), enter the same frozen room, and do the same tasks. Between subjects, eight per group, growing to twelve if the effects are small.

Which tasks. One per difficulty, each pushed to the point where its solution is needed and built so that without the solution the information cannot be reached: a pile of records on one thing (Supersede), a note said at one bench and needed at another (Route), several projects on one bench with one dimmed card that concerns you (Audience), five people's notes in one zone with a conflict between two of them (Authorship), and the nudged camera, where the last note is literally true and nobody said anything (Trace). Plus the zero-load control, one card on one thing, and the arrival scene, five minutes in a full room. Resume and trail are the same room under two prompts, split across participants. The tasks come from the shared sheet, dimension by dimension; the crossings table's partials say what must not be staged together.

How a session runs. An hour. Arrival first. Six task blocks, order counterbalanced, each observed for the action, the time, the check before acting, and a short cued debrief on what they concluded and why. The trust probes sit inside the blocks. At the end everyone leaves word for the next person and decides whether to release their own trace. Findings are reported per task, proportions correct with the test that fits, time and workload with repeated-measures statistics and effect sizes, and the debriefs coded.

The leaver side, optional. The claim that speaking in place costs less than Slack and yields records the pipeline can use is stated from the design and the pipeline benchmark. If time allows, a small seeding study makes it a measured claim: four to six people, thirty to forty minutes of real lab work in each of the two conditions, speak in place versus phone and Slack; capture is real (microphone, position, time), confirmation is simulated, no display is needed. It reports notes per hour, words per note, time off task, four-element completeness and workload, and its recordings become the seed room and the benchmark corpus. If it is skipped, the room is seeded by the team through the live pipeline and the leaver side is a stated limitation.

Questions. Whether YY and Darren accept the Slack group after the August agreement on a single group. Whether the IRB needs a modification for the design change. Whether one stale-top-card probe goes into the pile task. Between subjects as planned, or two matched wings of the lab for a within-subjects design if the pilots show small effects. Whether the leaver study runs at all, and if so before or after the arriver pilots.
