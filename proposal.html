Proposal and Paper Outline (September 2026 replan)

The paper in plain language, then the section-by-section outline and the decisions behind it. Written on the assumption that the system runs on RayNeo glasses; the Quest alternative is noted where it changes something. Companion to Framing (theory), Concept (mechanisms) and Plan (study). Dated 2026-09-18.

In one breath: workspaces used in shifts lose the small facts that only matter at the thing, because leaving word costs the leaver everything and pays them nothing. Spatial Memory lets the room carry its own past on two channels, what people said in place and where their bodies went, kept current by a Memory Graph, on glasses that open their camera only when the wearer stops. A week-long scenario shot on the running system shows the mechanisms work. A controlled study puts real arrivers into the room, against the same information delivered over Slack, on the situations that make a shared room hard.

1Background

Many people share one physical space without being there at the same time. Maker labs, research labs and studios run in shifts, one group in the morning and another in the afternoon. What hurts in such places is not the big decisions, which have meetings and documents. It is the small facts: someone swapped the printer nozzle, re-zeroed the scale, nudged the camera rig. Each of these matters only at the moment a later person reaches the thing it concerns, and by then the person who knows has gone.

2The problem

Face to face, you learn what others did in three ways. You watch them work, you hear them talk, and you read the state of the things they touched. Once the actor has left, the first two are gone and only the third remains, and the state of a thing tells you the present without the past. A nudged camera rig looks exactly like a calibrated one.

Why does the person who knows not leave word? Because leaving word is expensive. They must stop the task, pick up a phone, open a channel, choose a recipient and translate "this one" into words, and the payoff goes to someone else, later. Under that cost structure silence is the rational choice, so this kind of knowledge keeps dying. The theory behind this, workspace awareness (Gutwin and Greenberg) and the cost ledger of grounding (Clark and Brennan), is on the Framing tab.

3What we propose

Let the room carry its own past, on two channels.

The spoken channel. Standing at the thing, you say one sentence. No recipient, no description of where, because the place supplies both. The room pins the sentence there, and the next person who walks up sees it. This is authored feedthrough: the content is what you meant to say, the delivery is the place. It answers the artifact-side questions of the past: how did this come to be in this state, and when.

The body channel. The glasses you wear record where you went and where you stopped, without cameras, from inertial sensing between stops and one short camera look when you stop. At the end of the session you decide whether to release the trace. If you do, a later person can ask who was here, where a person has been, and who passed this spot. This is released trace: the consequential channel, watching bodies work, restored after the fact. It answers the person-side questions of the past, including the one nobody thought to say.

The Memory Graph. A layer of structure keeps both channels true as the room changes. A newer note folds the older one behind it. A project's note follows the work to the next bench. A note reaches only the people it concerns. Every note carries its author. On arrival, the room hands you the few items that matter today. The graph does mechanically what a person would otherwise have to do by re-reading everything, which is what Gutwin and Greenberg call awareness maintenance.

The hardware stance. Everything runs on glasses that keep the camera off. Between stops, position comes from inertial odometry. When the wearer stops, the camera takes a one-to-two-second look to relocalize against a one-time scan of the room, then turns off. Nobody is filmed, and the trace is a path with stations, not a video.

4How we test it

The paper has to show two things: the mechanisms work, and real people get the picture. The first is the scenario's job. We stage a week in the maker lab on the running system and film it, one beat per mechanism, including the beat where nobody said anything and only the trace can answer. The second is the study's job.

The study freezes one room with about thirty-four notes and casts makerspace-experienced outsiders as the newcomer. The tasks are not chosen ad hoc. Each pushes one of the four things that make a room hard to its extreme: ten layers of notes on one spot, one job's needs scattered over ten spots, four projects on one bench, five people's notes interleaved in one zone, plus a zero-load control task and a five-minute arrival scene. Half the participants use Spatial Memory. The other half receive the same notes as messages in a Slack channel on a phone, which is what people do today, and then do the same tasks in the same room.

5How a session runs

One hour per person. Five minutes on arrival to find out what matters today. Six task blocks, each observed for four things: was the action right, how long did it take, did they verify before acting, and how do they explain their decision afterwards. Participants in the Spatial Memory arm are also probed on three trusts: that the top note is the current one, that dimmed notes are not theirs, and that a signature is worth believing. In the last five minutes everyone leaves word for the next person and makes a real decision about releasing their own trace.

Two small technical results accompany the study. A benchmark of the structuring pipeline reports how often the speech-to-card step segments, anchors, files and links notes correctly, and how often a card needs correction. A validity paragraph reports how reliably the trace assigns stops to stations in our room. Neither is ablated or compared to prior systems. The odometry and relocalization components are cited, not evaluated.
