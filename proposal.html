Proposal

The paper in five blocks. Each block gives, in plain language and in logical order, what the section will say, then the questions still open. Written 2026-09-22 for the glasses version. The concept flow is on the Concept tab and the study derivation is on the shared sheet.

In one line. A room used in shifts loses the small facts that matter only at the thing, because leaving word costs the leaver everything and pays them nothing. Spatial Memory lets the room keep three kinds of memory, what was said at a thing, what a project needs next, and who was where. Five solutions keep them true as the room fills up. It runs on glasses that open the camera only when the wearer stops. A week-long scenario shows it runs, and a study against Slack shows that arrivers get a correct picture.

1Intro

Many people share one physical workspace without sharing time. Maker labs, research labs and studios run in shifts. What gets lost there is not the big decisions, which have meetings and documents, but the small facts that matter only when a later person reaches the thing. The nozzle was swapped. The scale was re-zeroed. The camera rig was nudged.

Face to face you learn what others did in three ways. You watch them, you hear them, and you read the state of the things they touched. Once the other person has left, the first two are gone and the third shows the present, not the past. A nudged rig looks calibrated.

The person who knows does not leave word because leaving word is expensive in every way Clark and Brennan's cost ledger names. You have to stop what you are doing and pick up a phone. You have to decide who needs it, and often nobody in particular does. You have to put into words where and what, when a gesture at the thing would have said it. The channel is not the place, so the reader has to rebuild the place from words. The payoff goes to someone else, later, while the cost is yours now. Under that ledger silence is the rational choice, and the small facts keep dying.

We propose Spatial Memory, a room that keeps its own past in three kinds of memory. What was said at a thing stays at the thing, one sentence with no recipient and no description of where, because the place supplies both. What a project needs next follows the project's work to its next place. Who was where is recorded by the wearer's own glasses and released by the wearer, the one kind nobody has to author. Together they answer the six past-tense questions of workspace awareness. As the room fills up, five things make those answers hard to keep true, and we give one solution for each. It runs live on glasses that open the camera only when the wearer stops.

What we did, and the contributions. We shot a week in a maker lab on the running system, ran a study with sixteen to twenty-four arrivers against the same notes delivered over Slack on tasks that push each difficulty to its limit, and benchmarked the speech-to-card step. If it runs, a small leaver study seeds the room and measures what speaking in place costs. Three contributions follow. The concept, which carries workspace awareness across time through the place itself, derived from the six questions to three kinds of memory to five difficulties and their solutions, each grounded in prior work. The system on glasses, with a released body trace as the memory nobody authors, shown running in the scenario. The study against Slack with the benchmark.

Positioning sentence, two candidates. Against a system. AsyncReality replays what the room did while you were away, and Spatial Memory lets the people who were there say what the next person needs to know, and remembers who they were. Against a class. AR annotation asks where to put a note, and Spatial Memory asks what a room should do with a week of them.

Questions. Which positioning sentence, after the positioning table is drawn. Whether the intro lists the six questions or leaves them to the concept. Whether the camera property belongs in the intro or only in the system section. The final numbers for the "what we did" paragraph. Whether "presence memory" reads as a kind of memory to a cold reader.

2Concept

The section is one derivation in four steps. What the later person needs to know. Who can supply it, and how, which gives three kinds of memory. What breaks them as the room fills, which gives five difficulties. What restores each, which gives five solutions. Every step cites the work it rests on, so the concept is a synthesis, not a coinage.

Step 1, what the later person needs to know. Gutwin and Greenberg list six past-tense questions of workspace awareness, quoted as they stand. In a room used in shifts the actor is gone, so watching and hearing are cut and only the state of things remains, which shows the present. The six questions are what the present cannot show.

Artifact historyHow did this artifact come to be in this state?the thing
Event historyWhen did that event happen?the thing
Action historyHow did that operation happen?an act, said or unsaid
Presence historyWho was here, and when?a person
Location historyWhere has a person been?a person
Action historyWhat has a person been doing?a person

Step 2, who can supply it, and how. Only the person who was there can answer, and there are two ways an answer can reach the room. They can say it, or the room can record what their body gave off. What is said can be anchored to two things, the thing it concerns or the work it belongs to. Crossing the two ways with the two anchors gives three kinds of memory and one empty cell.

The base of all three is delivery where the fact applies. A note said at the thing needs no recipient and no description of where, because the place supplies both, and a note said while walking lands on the thing it names, else where it was said, else at the nearest station with a flag. Together the three are Spatial Memory, the six questions answered in the room. Prior work supplies each piece and none of the whole. Notes anchored in place are AR annotation and situated notes (AnchorNote, Luo's placement studies). Records that follow a project are threads. Body traces released for others are the ubicomp presence line and PEARL's in-situ movement analysis.

Step 3, what breaks them as the room fills. On a quiet day the three memories answer the six questions. Each difficulty is a count that rises with use, and each threatens one question.

Step 4, what restores each. One solution per difficulty, each a relation kept on the record and a way of showing it at the place. The relations are the metadata of any messaging tool. Slack has versions, threads, audiences and signatures. What is new is that they take effect at the place, and that the fifth is a relation no channel can hold.

The first four work on what was said. The fifth works on what was recorded, in four steps of its own. Record, with inertial odometry between stops and a short camera look when the wearer stops. Release, the wearer's decision at the end of the session. Segment, so that stops become presence at stations and walks become transits past things. Surface, in the same room. The trace knows who passed, not what happened. Speaking is consent by itself, and the trace needs an explicit release. Collecting along a relation (a project's notes, a person's trail, the recent) and picking out what is for you on arrival follow from the relations and are described with the system.

Length, since AsyncReality's concept is two pages.

3Scenario

A week in the maker lab, shot on the running system. Monday, Sam seeds the room with a note at the printer, the rig and the shelf, and one said while walking about the blocked aisle. Tuesday, Renata swaps the nozzle back and her note folds over Sam's, a box moves to the shelf, and the watch logs on the shelf. Wednesday, Iris reads a rig note that is literally true and no longer right, because Dae-ho carried boxes past the tripod and said nothing, and the capture fails. Thursday, the room shows at the rig who passed after the last note, the cure-time card said at the glue bench waits at the sanding bench marked read first, and Iris checks before acting. Friday, the week's project notes go with the project. Each beat exercises one solution and a closing table says which. The plot stays as written in August. Thursday changes, one beat is added, and the illustrations become captures.

4System

The section mirrors the concept's derivation. What the base needs, what each kind of memory needs to get in, what each solution needs to be kept and shown, and where all of it runs. Nothing here is new engineering except the recorded input, and the section says so.

Assumptions. A workspace with fixed stations (benches, machines, shelves) that do not move between visits. One scan of the room, done once, gives the map and the station anchors. Moving objects are out of scope. Every person wears their own glasses, which have a camera that can be switched on and off and no positioning of their own, so all positioning below is ours.

The base, delivery where the fact applies. Cards live at anchors. A card is placed at its anchor plus a fixed offset, faces the viewer, and never covers a panel or a work surface. From afar an anchor shows a dot, on approach the cards, and several cards at one anchor fan out. Placement is a rule, not an optimization, because a card is one sentence read once, not a document read repeatedly.

Said memory gets in. The wearer says a wake word and one sentence, and the glasses record audio, position and time. Live transcription and an LLM turn the breath into cards, propose the anchor (the thing the sentence names, else where it was said, else the nearest station with a flag) and fill the fields, object or project, who it is for, warning or not, who, when. The LLM chooses only among the anchors the scan produced. The card appears at the place within seconds and the speaker confirms or fixes it there. Object and project memory differ only in the anchor the LLM proposes.

Recorded memory gets in. Between stops the wearer's position comes from inertial odometry on the glasses (InrtlAR). At a stop the camera turns on for one or two seconds, a few frames are matched against the scan, position and heading are corrected, and the camera turns off. The odometry guards the camera, since a match that lands far from where the odometry says the wearer is gets rejected, and carries the wearer through failed matches. At the end of the session the path is smoothed with every stop as a fixed point, stops become presence at stations, walks become transits past things, and the wearer decides whether to release it. This is the one part of the system that is ours to build rather than to assemble.

Solutions are kept and shown. Records, stations and people are stored as a plain graph with the five relations as edges. This is a design decision matched to the five solutions, not a contribution, and any messaging tool keeps the same thing under the hood.

What is for me on arrival needs no solution of its own, since it is audience and read-first collected at the door.

Where it runs. The glasses carry the microphone, the IMU, the camera bursts and the display. A phone in the pocket runs the odometry, the matching, the transcription, the LLM calls and the graph. Nothing runs after the fact except the benchmark. A table lists, for every feature, whether it is live on the glasses, live on the phone, or wizarded in the study.

Numbers the section reports. One benchmark table for the speech-to-card step, with segmentation, anchor proposal, object versus project, audience and warning, links and per-card corrections, against a rule-based and a manual ground truth. One paragraph on the trace in our room, with station attribution, dwell error, anchor hits for notes said while walking, the share of time the camera was on, and the one contrast that answers why not just photograph each stop. Odometry and matching are cited components and are not ablated or compared to prior systems.

Questions. RayNeo details to confirm on the device, which are IMU access and rate, frame access when toggled, world-anchored text, and phone link bandwidth. Matching against the scan without markers, or AprilTags at the stations, or both. Ground truth for the trace paragraph, a Quest worn alongside or a lab mocap. Whether the on-the-spot confirmation is a tap, a word, or nothing.

5Studies

Two roles, two studies, each with the baseline that fits its side. The task derivation lives on the shared sheet, Design x Study Dimensions (Dimensions, Intersections, task cells, task pool). The leaver study asks whether people put the past into the room when it costs one breath, against the way they do it today. The arriver study asks whether a person who arrives gets a correct picture out, against the same notes without the five solutions. The scenario has already shown the system runs, so neither study is about usability.

Study 1, the leaver. Four to six makerspace-experienced people work in the lab for thirty to forty minutes in each of two conditions, speaking in place or using a phone and Slack, order counterbalanced, with planted situations worth a note. Capture is real, with a microphone and position, confirmation is simulated, and no display is needed. Measures are notes per hour, words per note, time off task, whether a record names its thing, place, time and who it is for, and workload. The recordings seed the arriver room and feed the benchmark. If it is skipped, the team seeds the room through the live pipeline and the leaver side is a stated limitation.

Study 2, the arriver. The question is about awareness, not effort. PaperToPlace, the nearest study in shape, promises less effort and measures usability, workload, time and head movement, never whether the cook did the right thing. Our promise is the picture, so we measure first the action against a scripted truth, second how they got there (checked before acting, which cards opened, which route walked), third whether they accepted the room's judgments (the top card is current, dimmed cards are not theirs, a signature is worth believing, someone passed here after the last note), and last usability, workload and preference.

Against what. The same notes at the same anchors without the five solutions, every version visible, nothing carried, nothing dimmed, no names, no trace. This isolates what the paper adds, since delivery at the thing is already established by prior work, and it is the comparison the approved IRB protocol describes. Slack stays the baseline on the leaver side, where today's way is the right contrast. Both groups wear the glasses, enter the same prepared room and do the same tasks. Between subjects, eight per group, growing to twelve if the effects are small.

Which tasks. One per difficulty, each pushed to the point where its solution is needed and built so that without the solution the information cannot be reached. A pile of records on one thing. A note said at one bench and needed at another. Several projects on one bench with one dimmed card that concerns you. Five people's notes in one zone with a conflict between two of them. The nudged camera, where the last note is literally true and nobody said anything. Plus a control task with one card on one thing, and an arrival scene, five minutes in a full room. Resume and trail are the same room under two prompts, split across participants. The tasks come from the shared sheet, and the crossings table says what must not be staged together.

How a session runs. An hour. Arrival first. Six task blocks in counterbalanced order, each observed for the action, the time, the check before acting, and a short debrief on what they concluded and why. The trust questions sit inside the blocks. At the end everyone leaves word for the next person and decides whether to release their own trace. Findings are reported per task, with proportions correct and the test that fits, time and workload with repeated-measures statistics and effect sizes, and the debriefs coded.

Questions. Whether Study 1 runs at all, and before or after the arriver pilots. Re-alignment with YY and Darren, who agreed in August on a single group. Whether one stale top card goes into the pile task. Between subjects as planned, or two matched wings of the lab for a within-subjects design if the pilots show small effects.
